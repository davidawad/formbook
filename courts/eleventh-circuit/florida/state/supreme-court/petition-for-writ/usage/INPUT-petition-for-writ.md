# JURISDICTIONAL STATEMENT

Petitioner Edward Thomas James was sentenced to death on August 18, 1995. This petition presents issues which directly concern the constitutionality of his death sentences and is an original action under Fla. R. App. P. 9.100(a). This Court has original jurisdiction under Fla. R. App. P. 9.030(a)(3) and Art. V, § 3(b)(1) and § 3(b)(9), Fla. Const.

The Florida Constitution guarantees that “[t]he writ of habeas corpus shall be grantable of right, freely and without cost.” Art. I, § 13, Fla. Const.

Where a trial has resulted in “incongruous and manifestly unfair results,” an appellate court has the “inherent authority to grant a writ of habeas corpus.”

Stephens v. State, 974 So. 2d 455, 457 (Fla. 2d DCA 2008).

# PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

This Court has previously recognized that it has the “power to reconsider and correct erroneous rulings [made in earlier appeals] in exceptional circumstances and where reliance on the previous decision would result in manifest injustice.” State v. Akins, 69 So. 3d 261, 268 (Fla. 2011) (quoting Muehleman v. State, 3 So. 3d 1149, 1165 (Fla. 2009))

(alteration in original).

Such exceptional circumstances are present here. Mr. James’ death sentences are the byproduct of fundamental state and federal constitutional errors, including violations of the Sixth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments.

But those multiple violations went uncorrected because of a now-defunct Florida rule that allowed Mr. James—now known to have been suffering from an insidious dementing process at the time of his postconviction waiver—to languish without counsel for over a decade. This rule, coupled with the state courts’ arbitrary refusal to reinstate Mr. James’ appeals despite allowing reinstatement for similarly situated individuals, permanently frustrated Mr. James’ ability to obtain substantive review of his constitutional claims and violated his right to due process.

In light of the incongruous and manifestly unjust result that would ensue from Mr. James’ execution under these circumstances, this Court has the authority to utilize its inherent equitable authority and grant a writ of habeas corpus.

# GROUNDS FOR HABEAS CORPUS RELIEF

I.

This Court should reconsider its prior timeliness rulings, which unjustly precluded Mr. James from receiving merits review of the myriad constitutional claims he raised in postconviction.

Where a state provides for capital postconviction review, the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, which ensures fundamental fairness in litigation, applies to those proceedings. See Dist. Attorney’s Office v. Osborne, 557 U.S. 52, 69 (2009). Although “due process does not ‘dictat[e] the exact form such assistance must assume[,]” id. (quoting Pennsylvania v. Finley, 481 U.S. 551, 559 (1987)), postconviction relief procedures must “compor[t] with fundamental fairness[.]” Finley, 481 U.S. at 556; see also Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387, 400-01 (1985) (where, as in providing postconviction review, “a State opts to act in a field where its action has significant discretionary elements,” it must “act in accord with the dictates of the Constitution – and, in particular, in accord with the Due Process Clause”).

Here, the ends of due process warrant reconsideration of this Court’s rulings regarding the timeliness of Mr. James’ ineffective assistance of counsel and competency claims for multiple reasons. First, this Court’s timeliness rulings are largely the product of Mr. James’ longstanding deprivation of counsel, which by this Court’s own holding would never occur today. Second, the timeliness rulings are the product of inconsistent and arbitrary state-court action regarding the treatment of postconviction waivers.

And third, these timeliness rulings are inextricable from the substantive issue of Mr. James’ incompetency, which has been further demonstrated by the recent disclosure of brain scans indicating a decades-long dementing process.

The combined effect of these circumstances is an extraordinary situation in which—absent this Court’s intervention—there is no adequate state remedy to protect Mr. James’ constitutional rights. This Court should exercise its inherent equitable authority and grant a writ of habeas corpus.

A. This Court has recently recognized that the rule which allowed Mr. James to discharge his counsel is contrary to this Court’s precedent and the aims of justice.

In 2022, this Court recognized that the language of the rule which permitted Mr. James to discharge his counsel may be “inconsistent with this Court’s case law[.]” See In re Amendments to Florida Rules of Criminal Procedure 3.851 and Florida Rule of Appellate Procedure 9.142, 351 So. 3d 574, 575 (Fla. 2022) (citing Davis v. State, 257 So. 3d 100, 107 n.8 (Fla. 2018)). In response, this Court, sua sponte, considered amending Fla. R.

Crim. P. 3.851 (including prohibiting the discharge of postconviction counsel, even in cases of waiver) and ultimately amended the rules to reflect that “a capital defendant may waive pending postconviction proceedings but not postconviction counsel, and that a subsequent postconviction motion is allowable to raise certain specified claims after a waiver of pending postconviction proceedings.” In re Amendments, 351 So. 3d at 574 (emphasis added). This Court also amended Rule 9.142(d) by specifically removing any reference to the “discharge” or “discharging” of counsel.

In reaching this decision, this Court grappled with fundamental principles of death penalty jurisprudence the scope of postconviction waivers, and the importance of a capital defendant having counsel throughout all stages of a capital case. During oral argument, Chief Justice Canady stated:

We started on this journey…with Davis. That raised some issues about the scope of a defendant’s waiver of claims….One view is that…if you’re going to waive proceedings, that’s once and for all and forever. That’s not the position the court took. We thought that was not what we wanted. The majority did not want that.

See 2/10/22 Oral Argument, In re Amendments, Case No. SC21-537, at 9:13-9:53. These expressed values contrast with the admonishments given and actions taken by the state courts in Mr. James’ case.

Put simply, this Court has recognized that—notwithstanding a postconviction waiver—a death-sentenced individual must retain collateral counsel to protect his access to the courts and ability to avail himself of future claims. By today’s standards, Mr. James would not have gone unrepresented for any length of time, let alone years. But Mr. James was on his own for over a decade.

Justice requires that Mr. James not be faulted for his inability to access the court system during this time; nor can he be expected to have discovered newly available claims, conducted investigation, or litigated his own incompetency. This Court should exercise its inherent equitable power to revisit its prior timeliness rulings regarding Mr. James’ postconviction claims; and, having done so, conduct a robust merits review of his previously-barred constitutional claims.

B.

Similarly situated individuals to Mr. James have been allowed to reinstate their postconviction proceedings.

Declining to revisit this Court’s prior timeliness rulings would work a manifest injustice and result in arbitrariness, because other death-sentenced individuals have been permitted to reobtain counsel and reinstate their postconviction proceedings after a simple change of mind.

For instance, Robert Trease, whose death sentence became final on November 15, 2000, successfully dismissed his collateral proceedings and counsel and had both reinstated after changing his mind at least three times.

First, Trease moved to dismiss counsel in March 2001 before the oneyear statute of limitations for a postconviction motion had lapsed, prompting counsel to file a shell motion for postconviction relief questioning his competency. Trease v. State, 41 So. 3d 119, 120-21 (Fla. 2010). After a hearing, Trease was determined competent, and his collateral proceedings were dismissed in May 2001 and his counsel discharged. Id. Approximately one year later in June 2002, Trease “changed his mind” and, at his request, the postconviction court reappointed counsel and reinstated the earlier postconviction motion in October 2002. Id.; see also Trease v. Sec’y, Dep’t of Corrs. et al, No. 8:11-cv-233-SDM-TGW, ECF No. 26 at 1.

After a postconviction evidentiary hearing was held, but prior to issuance of a final order, Trease again moved to dismiss his collateral proceedings and counsel. The trial court informed Trease that it would not remove counsel until the postconviction motion was resolved. Trease, 41 So. 3d at 121. The court denied relief on Trease’s postconviction motion in May 2007, then held a hearing on Trease’s motion to waive before, again, dismissing Trease’s collateral proceedings and counsel. Id.

In July 2007, state counsel appealed the denial of the postconviction motion. Id. Trease moved to discharge his counsel in the trial court, which was dismissed for a lack of jurisdiction. Id. Trease, again, moved to dismiss counsel and waive his appeals in May 2008—this time in this Court, which relinquished jurisdiction so that the trial court could conduct a Durocher hearing. Id. Trease’s motion was granted, and this Court affirmed the waivers after postconviction counsel appealed. Id. at 123.4 Similarly, Victor Farr, another death-sentenced Florida prisoner, repeatedly waived collateral proceedings and counsel and sought their reinstatement after changing his mind. After Farr’s second resentencing proceeding, and before this Court affirmed his new death sentence, Farr moved to waive his collateral proceedings and dismiss counsel. See Farr v. State, No. 91-0002-CF, Waiver of Right to Seek Post-Conviction Relief (Third Judicial Circuit for Columbia County, Fla., Jan. 13, 1994). After a hearing, Farr’s motion was granted and his collateral proceedings and counsel were discharged. Id., Order (Oct. 17, 1996).

In Mr. James’ case, this Court upheld the dismissal of his 3.851 proceedings on the grounds that his claims were untimely, he failed to satisfy an exception under Fla. R. Crim. Failure to revisit this Court’s timeliness rulings—which directly resulted from the deprivation of counsel and failure to reinstate proceedings that is entirely at odds with the state courts’ actions in Farr and Trease—would render Mr. James’ execution arbitrary and capricious.

C.

Newly disclosed brain scans shed new light on the invalidity of Mr. James’ prior waivers and undermine the state courts’

justifications for denying substantive postconviction review.

On January 11, 2023, Mr. James suffered near fatal cardiac arrest while incarcerated at Union Correctional Institution. He was found unresponsive in his cell and blue in color See Successive 3.851 Motion, Florida v. James, No. 1993-CF-3237 (Seminole Co., Feb. 23, 2025) at 9. It is unknown how long he had been unconscious and without oxygen before he was found. Id. He required several rounds of resuscitation in the prison before being transferred to UF Health Gainesville, including multiple rounds of shocks delivered via an automated external defibrillator and followed by compressions for more than twenty minutes, as well as intubation in the field Id. The compressions resulted in multiple rib fractures. Upon Mr. James’

hospitalization, additional lifesaving measures were taken, including continued intubation, therapeutic hypothermia, and the placement of a cardiac stent. Id. at 9-10. His lack of consciousness resulted in a loss of oxygen-saturated blood to his brain causing an acute encephalopathy, or brain injury. Id.

In addition to the loss of oxygen to his brain, medical staff noted that the comatose Mr. James had obtained an acute head injury. Id. Mr. James remained in a profound coma, with no immediate signs of neurological recovery, for two days before showing initial improvement. Id. at 13. During this time, a CT scan of his head and cervical spine was obtained to gauge the extent of Mr. James’ altered mental state and encephalopathy, and to determine whether they resulted from presumably striking the back of his head when he fell during the cardiac event. Had Mr. James not suffered from a near-fatal medical catastrophe, he would never have been able to obtain such neuroimaging. After two years of his counsel’s diligent efforts to obtain the scans, they were disclosed four days before the signing of Mr. James’

execution warrant. Id. at 14-16.

Subsequent review by Dr. Erin Bigler, Ph.D., indicated evidence of “cerebral atrophy” in the frontoparietal realm, including significant space in which brain tissue should be present, but there is only cerebrospinal fluid.

See, e.g., id. at 11. At the time Mr. James’ imaging was conducted—the day of his cardiac arrest—cerebral atrophy due to anoxic brain injury (i.e., the deprivation of oxygen Mr. James suffered before resuscitation) would not yet have shown up on a CT scan. Id. Thus, although the scans were responsive to Mr. James’ cardiac arrest and subsequent head injury, the brain atrophy reflected “predated [his] cardiac arrest, possibly by many years” and reflects a “chronic brain condition” wherein the structural brain changes may have begun during his juvenile period. Id. at 11 (emphasis added); see also id. at 13.

This new information sheds critical light on the state courts’ prior rulings, including the timeliness rulings that precluded substantive review of the competency claims and multiple ineffective assistance of counsel claims related to Mr. James’ mental state and plea/waiver actions. As has been discovered in the years since Mr. James’ last appeal to this Court, he suffers from a neurodegenerative disorder—inherently a progressive, dementing condition. See id. at 8, 14. And when this is taken into account with what was already known about Mr. James’ substance abuse, life history, and mental health, it changes the picture of his case such that this Court’s reconsideration is necessary. See Appendix to Successive 3.851 Motion, Florida v. James, No. 1993-CF-3237 (Seminole Co., Feb. 23, 2025) at 14 (any of Mr. James’ previously known vulnerabilities “alone can increase the risk of developing dementia later in life…but their combined presence significantly strengthens the correlation, suggesting a more substantial contribution to his cognitive decline.”); id. (comparing Mr. James’ brain to “a rusted engine that is suddenly pushed to his limits. The underlying structural weaknesses, previously stable but compromised, are now far more susceptible to rapid deterioration, causing the engine to fail much sooner than it otherwise would have.”).

The new evidence demonstrates the extent of Mr. James’ cognitive dysfunction—which is much greater than any court has recognized—and indicates that the degenerative/dementing process may have begun to take root close to 50 years ago. This, in turn, undermines all of this Court’s postconviction rulings regarding timeliness and competency, and warrants reconsideration of those rulings.

D.

Failure to revisit this Court’s prior timeliness rulings would work a manifest injustice.

Since the time of this Court’s prior rulings in Mr. James’ case, this Court has recognized the problematic effect of the rule which allowed Mr. James to discharge his postconviction counsel and go without representation for many subsequent years. Also, since the time of those rulings, new information about Mr. James’ longstanding dementia has rebutted many core assumptions underlying the prior determinations of competency in his case.

In the background, the state circuit courts have arbitrarily and inconsistently enforced purportedly competent postconviction waivers like the one Mr. James entered in 2003, and in Mr. James’ case specifically, the state circuit court provided misinformation about the effects of such a waiver.

When these developments are viewed in tandem, the validity of Mr. James’ trial-level pleas and waivers is undermined; his trial counsel’s ineffectiveness in abandoning a viable guilt-phase defense and providing experts with comprehensive mitigation information takes on a greater significance; the trial court’s improper rejection of the EED statutory mitigator is recast; and the vastness of Mr. James’ impairments at the time of his postconviction waiver and discharge of counsel is especially stark. This Court’s “reliance on [its] previous decision[s] would result in manifest injustice.” State v. Akins, 69 So. 3d 261, 268 (Fla. 2011) (quoting Muehleman v. State, 3 So. 3d 1149, 1165 (Fla. 2009)). This Court should reconsider its prior timeliness rulings and allow for merits review of Mr. James’

constitutional claims.

# CONCLUSION AND RELIEF SOUGHT

This Court should grant Mr. James’ petition for habeas corpus, stay his execution, and grant appropriate relief.
