# INTRODUCTION

Respondent Department of Health ("DOH") filed an administrative complaint against Petitioner Mark DeLuca, M.D. ("Dr. DeLuca") for alleged sexual misconduct. Over a thirteen-month period, the DOH conducted and closed its investigation, submitted its finalized and complete investigative report to a Probable Cause Panel (the "Panel"), received authorization from the Panel to issue an administrative complaint based on the investigative report, and issued not only an Administrative Complaint but an Amended Administrative Complaint against Dr. DeLuca. At no point during that investigative process did the DOH seek copies of text messages between Dr. DeLuca and the patient at issue.

One day after issuing the Amended Administrative Complaint, the DOH began issuing new "investigative" subpoenas (despite its investigation already being closed) to look for new evidence against Dr. DeLuca from T-Mobile and AT&T, primarily seeking text message correspondences. At that point, the DOH had not yet submitted this matter to the Florida Division of Administrative Hearings ("DOAH") despite Dr. DeLuca’s Requests for a Formal Administrative Hearing that he submitted to the DOH in response to both the Administrative Complaint and the Amended Administrative Complaint, and the DOH did not yet have subpoena powers under the APA.

The DOH, through its Affidavits for Subpoena, made false statements and misrepresentations to support the need and basis for the subpoenas. The DOH’s true purpose in issuing the subpoenas was to improperly obtain the content of text messages that the DOH did not obtain during its investigation and which the DOH cannot obtain without a warrant, and certainly not via a subpoena under applicable Florida law.

After it completed its investigation and before the DOH could obtain subpoena power by referring the case to DOAH, the DOH realized that it needed text messages and the content of them which the DOH had not bothered or even attempted to obtain during the investigation stage in the case. Therefore, and without any notice to Dr. DeLuca which is required in order to comply with his due process rights under applicable federal and Florida constitutional law, the DOH surreptitiously issued the subpoenas to AT&T and T-Mobile.

Moreover, once the DOH and its counsel obtained responsive records, including Dr. DeLuca’s cell phone records (but not the text messages themselves), the DOH and its counsel intentionally hid and withheld the fact that it had sought and obtained such records, and failed to provide copies of them to Dr. DeLuca’s counsel despite being required to do so pursuant to multiple written requests submitted by Dr. DeLuca and his counsel for a complete copy of the DOH’s investigative file.

Immediately after referring this matter to DOAH, and based upon information received in the subpoena responses, the DOH made a discovery request to Dr. DeLuca requesting - for the first time - copies of any and all text messages between Dr. DeLuca and the patient. At that point the DOH still had not revealed that they had issued subpoenas or that they had received responses to the subpoenas.

After the DOH’s request but prior to Dr. DeLuca’s response to same, Dr. DeLuca became aware of the subpoena’s existence. Pursuant to the exclusionary rule, Dr. DeLuca therefore objected to the DOH’s request which had been made based on information obtained from the improperly-issued and concealed subpoenas. The DOH filed a Motion to Compel, and the administrative law judge granted the Motion to Compel and denied Dr. DeLuca’s corresponding protective motions on April 18, 2025.

# BASIS FOR INVOKING JURISDICTION

Pursuant to § 120.68(1)(b), Fla. Stat., a preliminary, procedural, or intermediate agency action or ruling is immediately reviewable if review of the final agency decision would not provide an adequate remedy.

The proper method of seeking review of a nonfinal administrative order is to file a timely petition in the appellate court. See Fla. R. App. P. 9.100(c)(3). Pursuant to § 120.68(2)(a), Fla. Stat., such review of non-final agency action shall be sought through a petition in either the appellate district where the agency maintains its headquarters or where a party resides or as otherwise provided by law. The Order to be reviewed in this case was rendered on April 18, 2025, and this Petition is therefore timely under Fla. R. App. P. 9.100(c)(1).

Petitioner, as a resident of Palm Beach County, files this Petition seeking review of a non-final agency order compelling discovery of privileged information as well as constitutionally protected information. An order compelling discovery over a claim that the evidence is privileged is generally reviewable by an appellate court, because the harm cannot be remedied on review of the final order. See *State, Dept. of Transp. v. OHM Remediation Services Corp.*, 772 So. 2d 572 (Fla. 1st DCA 2000); *Scientific Games, Inc., v. Dittler Bros., Inc.*, 586 So. 2d 1128 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991); *Ranpura v. Department of Prof’l Regulation*, 507 So. 2d 146 (Fla. 1st DCA 1987).

# NATURE OF RELIEF SOUGHT

The nature of the relief sought is an order granting this Petition, and quashing the administrative Order dated April 18, 2025 granting the DOH’s Motion to Compel, and also reversing the portion of the Order denying Dr. DeLuca’s Motion to Suppress and Motion to Exclude and instead granting both Motions.

# ARGUMENT

Appellate review of the Order in question is the appropriate remedy based on the facts in this matter and the relief being sought, as the Order requires the disclosure of protected and privileged information. See *State, Dept. of Transp. v. OHM Remediation Services Corp.*, 772 So. 2d 572 (Fla. 1st DCA 2000); *Scientific Games, Inc., v. Dittler Bros., Inc.*, 586 So. 2d 1128 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991); *Ranpura v. Department of Prof'l Regulation*, 507 So. 2d 146 (Fla. 1st DCA 1987).

Similar to a petitioner seeking certiorari relief of a nonfinal order, a petitioner seeking review of nonfinal administrative action generally must establish that the action "departs from the essential requirements of law and thus causes material injury to the petitioner throughout the remainder of the proceedings, effectively leaving no adequate remedy on appeal." *Allstate Ins. Co. v. Langston*, 655 So. 2d 91, 95 (Fla. 1995); *Robles v. Baptist Health South Florida, Inc.*, 197 So. 3d 1196, 1199 (Fla. 3d DCA 2016).

In petitions for review of nonfinal administrative orders, the standard of review is essentially the same as that for nonfinal orders from civil proceedings. An order compelling discovery over a claim that the evidence is privileged is generally reviewable by an appellate court, because the harm cannot be remedied on review of the final order. *State Dep’t of Transp. v. OHM Remediation Servs. Corp.*, 772 So. 2d 572, 573 (Fla. 1st DCA 2000).

Although the DOH is permitted under Fla. Stat. § 456.071 to issue subpoenas during an investigation, there is no statutory authority that permits warrantless searches of phones or phone records. The investigation period ended months before the DOH’s issuance of the "investigative" subpoenas at issue and unquestionably by the time of the Panel’s finding of probable case and authorization to issue an administrative complaint. Further, while the DOH also has subpoena powers in proceedings in accordance with the APA, the DOH does not have such subpoena powers unless and until the DOH has referred the case to DOAH. The DOH did not refer this case to DOAH until months after issuing and receiving responses to the improper subpoenas. Therefore, the DOH did not have subpoena power or the legal authority to exercise it at the time either of the subpoenas were issued. Consequently, the subpoenas issued by the DOH were not valid or legal.

Moreover, a search warrant is needed to search such records. "Overbroad or unreasonable subpoenas, including investigative subpoenas, violate the Fourth Amendment protection against unreasonable searches and seizures. See *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43, 76, 26 S. Ct. 370 (1906) (overruled on other grounds but subsequently cited with approval in *Dean v. State*, 478 So.2d 38 (Fla.1985)). ‘The gist of the protection is ... that the disclosure sought shall not be unreasonable.’ *Okla. Press Publ’g Co. v. Walling*, 327 U.S. 186, 208 (1946); see also *Imparato v. Spicola*, 238 So. 2d 503, 511 (Fla. 2d DCA 1970)." *Check ’n Go of Florida, Inc. v. State*, 790 So. 2d 454, 459 (Fla. 5th DCA 2001).

The Florida Supreme Court spoke on the quantum of information that could be lawfully discovered through an administrative subpoena in *Dean v. State*, 478 So. 2d 38 (Fla. 1985). There, the court reaffirmed that the subpoena must be ‘properly limited in scope, relevant in purpose, and specific in directive,’ in order not to be unduly burdensome. *Id.* at 40. The Second District Court of Appeal amplified this principle in holding that "[a] subpoena duces tecum may not lawfully require the production of a mass of books and papers, merely so that one may search through them to gather evidence; and an omnibus subpoena for all, or even a substantial part, of the books and records of the subpoenaed party is invalid." *Imparato*, 238 So. 2d at 511. *Id.* at 459-460.

Probable cause for a search warrant exists when the affidavit in support of the warrant alleges sufficient facts to cause a person of reasonable caution to believe that contraband or other evidence of criminal activity is located at the place to be searched. Fla. Stat. § 933.02. The language in a warrant must be confined to a particular crime and does not grant broad access to law enforcement to essentially rummage for evidence of items that may or may not be a crime.

However, in direct contravention to this established legal principle, the DOH sought text messages and phone records for a 13-month period of time expanding months beyond the pertinent timeframe at issue in the case in hopes of finding some specific communication to support their case. Further, the DOH’s subpoena explicitly sought "any and all certified records for calls/phone detail records showing all communication" for Dr. DeLuca’s cellular phone during that 13-month period.[^2] Thus, the DOH was seeking the actual text messages and their content, not just records.

Although there are no Florida cases directly on point, there is ample federal precedent in this area. The warrant application in *United States v. Chavez*, 423 F. Supp. 3d 194 (W.D.N.C. 2019) sought to cast its net as broadly as the DOH in the instant case. The warrant in *Chavez* demanded the seizure of categories of records and data from the defendant's Facebook account – in effect, asking for the sum of information for the account.

Again, as in the application at bar, the application in *Chavez* contemplated that once this seizure was completed, law enforcement would conduct a search of the entire account, determining for itself what was reflective of the probable cause that engendered the search in the first place.[^3] Citing *United States v. Blake*, 868 F. 3d 960 (11th Cir. 2017), the *Chavez* court found this procedure to be unconstitutionally overbroad and inconsistent with the Fourth Amendment particularity requirement. *Chavez*, 423 F. Supp. 3d at 206-07.

## The Fourth Amendment Protects Against Unreasonable Search and Seizure

As a United States and Florida citizen, Dr. DeLuca had a right to be secure in his person, his home, his papers and his effects against unreasonable searches and seizures. That right is guaranteed both by the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution, and by Article I, §12, of the Florida Constitution. Furthermore, the Florida Constitution "shall be construed in conformity with the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution, as interpreted by the United States Supreme Court." Article I, §12, Florida Constitution (2024).

The exclusionary rule which stems from this Fourth Amendment right (more commonly referred to as the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine) applies in administrative proceedings when there are "circumstances involving ‘egregious violations of Fourth Amendment or other liberties that might transgress notions of fundamental fairness and undermine the probative value of the evidence obtained.’" *Valdez v. Dept. of Revenue, State*, 622 So. 2d 62, 63 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993) (quoting *Lopez-Medina*, 468 U.S. at 1050-1051).

While search warrants are generally expected to be issued prior to any search, certain exceptions have been recognized, including extraordinary circumstances. Where there is no search warrant, the prosecution has "the burden to establish that the evidence sought to be suppressed was obtained lawfully." *State v. Setzler*, 667 So. 2d 343, 345 (Fla. 1st DCA 1995).

The Fourth Amendment warrant requirement also presents a constitutional limitation to subpoena enforcement. If the Constitution requires a search warrant to obtain certain information, the government cannot use a subpoena to get that information instead. See, e.g., *Dean v. State*, 478 So. 2d 38, 42 (Fla. 1985) ("A subpoena duces tecum is not a substitute for a search warrant, nor may its use be a subterfuge for a warrantless search and seizure"). While a prosecutor can issue a subpoena pursuant to an ongoing investigation, a search warrant involves far more procedural and constitutional hurdles.

A search warrant requires the government to prepare a detailed search warrant application, including an affidavit from a law enforcement agent, to convince a judge that there is "probable cause to believe that the legitimate object of a search is located in a particular place." *Steagald v. United States*, 451 U.S. 204, 213 (1981). Additionally, the warrant must be narrowly tailored and describe with particularity the objects that will be searched and seized under the Fourth Amendment. See *Maryland v. Garrison*, 480 U.S. 79, 84 (1987). "The purpose behind the warrant requirement is to insure that the judgment of a neutral...magistrate is imposed against the unbridled discretion of an official caught up in the heat of an investigation." *United States v. Hermiz*, 42 F. Supp. 3d 856, 866 (E.D. Mich. 2014) (quoting *United States v. Blue Diamond Coal*, 667 F.2d 510, 518 (6th Cir. 1981)); see *United States v. Amerson*, 483 F.3d 73, 82 (2d Cir. 2007) ("[A] significant reason for warrants [is] to provide a check on the arbitrary use of government power").

The government must take basic and technical steps to obtain a search warrant. Most importantly, they must be able to establish probable cause and meet the other requirements of a search warrant for a phone. Where the government lacks probable cause authority to obtain a search warrant or suspects it will not be able to access a password-protected phone through a search warrant, its only option may be to seek information from the phone via subpoena. But that does not mean that the Fourth Amendment does not apply and limit the government's subpoena power.

## A Warrant Is Required to Obtain Text Messages

Supreme Court precedent makes it clear that the Fourth Amendment fully protects text messaging. Text messaging has become one of the predominant forms of communication in our society. Cell phones and cell phone records often contain a person's most private correspondence with friends, family, co-workers and even clients/patients. If the government wants to read those messages, a subpoena is not sufficient; it must get a warrant.

The Supreme Court has long held that a "Fourth Amendment search occurs when the government violates a subjective expectation of privacy that society recognizes as reasonable." *Kyllo v. United States*, 533 U.S. 27, 33 (2001). Decades ago, the Supreme Court established that a government invasion of private communications is a search that requires a warrant. *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347, 351 (1967). The court in *Katz* reaffirmed that the Fourth Amendment protects "people, not places," and it held that people have a reasonable expectation of privacy in their private telephone conversations. See 389 U.S. at 351.

Since the *Katz* decision in 1967, methods of communication have evolved with technology. With this evolution the federal courts have steadily expanded fundamental Fourth Amendment protections to other types of private correspondence. See *Kyllo*, 533 U.S. at 34 (evolving technology must not be permitted to "erode the privacy guaranteed by the Fourth Amendment"). For example, in *United States v. Jacobsen*, 466 U.S. 109, 114 (1984), the Supreme Court held that "[l]etters and other sealed packages are in the general class of effects in which the public at large has a legitimate expectation of privacy; warrantless searches of such effects are presumptively unreasonable."

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit in *United States v. Warshak* then extended the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement to email. 631 F. 3d 266, 288 (6th Cir. 2010) ("Given the fundamental similarities between email and traditional forms of communication, it would defy common sense to afford email lesser Fourth Amendment protection."). Private communication done by text message, rather than by phone call, letter, or email, is not any less protected.

The constitutionality of a warrantless search of a cell phone incident to arrest was addressed in *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373, 402 (2014). The Court held that, absent an applicable exception, a warrant is required to search a cell phone because an individual has a reasonable expectation of privacy in its contents. As the Supreme Court wrote, "[a] cell phone" is "an important feature of human anatomy" and "[t]he sum of an individual's private life can be reconstructed" through a search of a person's phone.

The Court further held:

> "Modern cell phones are not just another technological convenience. With all they contain and all they may reveal, they hold for many Americans 'the privacies of life[.]' The fact that technology now allows an individual to carry such information in his hand does not make the information any less worthy of the protection for which the Founders fought. Our answer to the question of what police must do before searching a cell phone seized incident to an arrest is accordingly simple—get a warrant."

*Id.* at 403. See also *Smallwood v. State*, 113 So. 3d 724, 736 (Fla. 2013) (quoting *State v. Smith*, 124 Ohio St. 3d 163, 920 N.E. 2d 949, 955 (2009)) ("Once the cell phone is in police custody, the state has satisfied its immediate interest in collecting and preserving evidence and can take preventive steps to ensure that the data found on the phone are neither lost nor erased. But because a person has a high expectation of privacy in a cell phone's contents, police must then obtain a warrant before intruding into the phone's contents."); *State v. Worsham*, 227 So. 3d 602, 604 (Fla. 4th DCA 2017) ("Modern technology facilitates the storage of large quantities of information on small, portable devices. The emerging trend is to require a warrant to search these devices.").

Four years later, in *Carpenter v. United States*, 138 S. Ct 2206, 2214 (2018), the Supreme Court squarely rejected an argument that the government has the power to subpoena data in which an individual has a reasonable expectation of privacy under the Fourth Amendment. In *Carpenter*, the government obtained an order under the Stored Communications Act for cell site location information from wireless carriers to track a defendant's location. Noting that such an order was akin to a subpoena, the court held that it was a violation of the defendant's Fourth Amendment rights, because the defendant had a reasonable expectation of privacy in his cumulative location data. *Id.* at 2212.

As relevant here, in response to an argument from Justice Samuel Alito (Alito, J., dissenting), the majority concluded that this type of data cannot be subpoenaed under existing Supreme Court precedent. It stated: "[T]his court has never held that the Government may subpoena third parties for records in which the suspect has a reasonable expectation of privacy." 138 S. Ct. at 2221 (citation omitted). The Court further noted that, "[i]f the choice to proceed by subpoena provided a categorical limitation on Fourth Amendment protection, no type of record would ever be protected by the warrant requirement." *Id.*

The Court dismissed the contention that "private letters, digital contents of a cell phone—any personal information reduced to document form, in fact—may be collected by subpoena for no reason other than 'official curiosity,'" and it stated that the "subpoena doctrine" should not be used to "overcom[e] any reasonable expectation of privacy." *Id.* at 2222 (emphasis added).

Taken together, *Riley* and *Carpenter* stand for the proposition that the government is required to obtain a warrant to search a cell phone because an individual has a reasonable expectation of privacy in the contents of the phone and the government may not use a subpoena to circumvent that warrant requirement. Appropriately, applying *Riley* and *Carpenter* to private text messages located on a personal cell phone or in third party records, the government is required to obtain a warrant to read them. It cannot compel an individual or third party to turn over private text messages by issuing a subpoena.

Given that advancing technology has led to a significant shift in private communication from the telephone and letter to email and text message, this extension of basic Fourth Amendment guarantees to these technologies should not be controversial. Just as the words one speaks into a phone are private, so too are the words one types into a phone.

In *Young v. State*, 394 So. 3d 1174 (Fla. 4th DCA 2024), the Fourth District Court of Appeals found the defendant had a reasonable expectation of privacy in his private social media messages and private subscriber information, and thus the detective's warrantless search of the defendant's private social media content during homicide investigation violated the Fourth Amendment. The detective had garnered evidence of defendant's involvement in homicide through defendant's private messages, which were analogous to cell phone's text messages. The same privacy concerns applied to private social media records as to cell phone data, and multiple federal courts have recognized reasonable expectation of privacy in private social media content.

Whether the government takes possession of the cell phone is irrelevant under the Fourth Amendment analysis. If a subpoena invades a reasonable expectation of privacy, such as by compelling an individual or third party to turn over the individual’s private text messages, the government has conducted a search. "A Fourth Amendment search occurs when the government violates a subjective expectation of privacy that society recognizes as reasonable." *Kyllo*, 533 U.S. at 33.

The *Carpenter* Court rejected the argument that a subpoena did "not involve the direct taking of evidence," and hence did not implicate the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement. 138 S. Ct. at 2221. The Court declined to adopt the view that subpoenas are "subject[] to lenient scrutiny without regard to the suspect's expectation of privacy."

The subpoenas issued by the DOH on their face clearly request both cellular phone communication providers to provide communication records including text messages. Significantly they do not exclude content, and in paragraphs 20-23 of the DOH’s Response to Motion to Quash the DOH confirms in no uncertain terms that the subpoenas were issued to substantiate that text messages were exchanged between Dr. DeLuca and Patient DB and that they were in fact "sexual in nature" – in other words, seeking the content of the text messages themselves. (App. 1882).

## The DOH Did Not Have Authority to Issue the Subpoenas

The subpoenas at issue sought "any and all certified records for calls/phone detail records showing all communication including text messages between the phone numbers stated on the corresponding Subpoenas from the timeframe of May 1, 2022, through May 31, 2023.[^4] Even if the DOH was authorized to issue a subpoena after the investigation was completed and after the file was transferred to the PSU - which it was not - the Subpoena and attached affidavit are facially invalid. Not only was the timeframe stated in the Subpoena overly broad and unduly burdensome, but it requested records that are subject to and prohibited by the Fourth Amendment’s and Article 1, Section 12’s rights against unreasonable searches and seizures.

In both of the subpoenas at issue, the DOH asserted that they were issued pursuant to § 456.071, Fla. Stat. That section explicitly states that the department shall have the power to issue subpoenas "[f]or the purpose of any investigation or proceeding conducted by the department."

The DOH did not issue the August 2024 and September 2024 subpoenas for the purpose of any investigation. As noted above, pursuant to the DOH’s own investigative report, the investigation in this matter was closed and final on September 25, 2023. (App. 10). Further, the completion of and legal sufficiency of the DOH’s investigation was required prior to the Panel’s June 28, 2024 consideration of this matter pursuant to § 456.073(2), Fla. Stat. ("When its investigation is complete and legally sufficient, the department shall prepare and submit to the probable cause panel of the appropriate regulatory board the investigative report of the department."). Accordingly – and necessarily - the DOH completed its investigation on September 25, 2023 and submitted its investigative report to the Panel at some point between September 25, 2023 and June 28, 2024.

The DOH also did not issue both subpoenas as part of a proceeding. Notably, the DOH explicitly asserted in the subpoenas and Affidavits for Subpoenas that the subpoenas are issued as part of an investigation. The proceeding with DOAH pursuant to the APA did not commence in this case until the DOH referred the case to DOAH in February 2025, nearly six months after it issued the improper subpoenas.

Further, the DOH served Dr. DeLuca with its Amended Administrative Complaint on August 20, 2024. The DOH initiated issuance of the subpoenas on the very next day, and the first such subpoena was issued a week later on August 28, 2024. At that time, Dr. DeLuca had not yet even served his EOR indicating that he would request a hearing for the Amended Administrative Complaint. This matter was not referred to DOAH until February 2025, six (6) months later.

In other words, after the issuance of an administrative complaint authorized by the Panel based on the Panel’s review of the DOH’s investigative report (which was statutorily required to be both complete and legally sufficient prior to its submittal to the Panel), the DOH then exceeded its authority and effectively reopened its investigation into Dr. DeLuca – without notice – in an attempt to obtain new evidence regarding the very same purported conduct that was the subject of the already-completed investigation.

At that point in the case, the investigation had already been concluded and the DOH did not have subpoena power yet under the APA. Nevertheless, the DOH had apparently realized that it did not have the evidence needed to prove the allegations against Dr. DeLuca by clear and convincing evidence at a formal administrative hearing. Therefore, the DOH chose to circumvent applicable Florida law and violate Dr. DeLuca’s federal and Florida constitutional and due process rights and issue the subpoenas in an effort to obtain authenticated copies of text messages between Dr. DeLuca and the patient from third parties: (1) after the close of the DOH’s investigation; (2) prior to the DOH referring this matter to DOAH and obtaining subpoena power under the APA as part of the proceedings under same; (3) without notice to Dr. DeLuca or any opportunity for Dr. DeLuca to challenge the subpoenas; (4) before any proceeding with DOAH pursuant to the APA had been initiated in this case; and (5) without a warrant.

The Investigative Services Unit ("ISU") is the division at the DOH which issued the subject subpoenas. From the DOH’s own website and brochure (App. 1837-1838) the role of the ISU is to conduct an investigation, which can include the issuance of subpoenas, and then send the legal division a copy of the final investigative report for review. All of these steps occurred in the investigation in this case prior to the case being presented to the Panel, with one glaring and highly notable exception - the subject subpoenas.

After the completion of the investigation by the ISU, the file is passed on to the Prosecution Services Unit ("PSU") to review the investigative file and recommend to the Panel whether to move forward with an administrative complaint or close the file without further action. At that point a case is submitted to the Panel, the investigation is complete. Upon the issuance of an administrative complaint, the role of the PSU is to prosecute the complaint based on the evidence within the completed investigative file. The PSU does not have the authority to send a file back to the ISU for further investigation, as the DOH did here, thereby exceeding its statutory and legal authority, and any request or need for further evidence to prosecute the administrative complaint should have been done via formal discovery requests to Dr. DeLuca in accordance with the APA or seeking an issuance of subpoenas under the APA.
