# I. BASIS FOR JURISDICTION

This Court has original jurisdiction to issue writs of prohibition to lower tribunals and judges under article V, section 4(b)(3) of the Florida Constitution and Fla. R. App. P. 9.030(b)(3). Prohibition is the proper method for reviewing an order denying a motion to disqualify a trial judge. *JJN FLB, LLC v. CFLB Partnership, LLC*, 283 So. 3d 922, 925 (Fla. 3d DCA 2019). The appellate court reviews the legal sufficiency of a motion to disqualify de novo. *Parker v. State*, 3 So. 3d 974, 982 (Fla. 2009).

> “A writ of prohibition is the proper procedure for appellate review to test the validity of a motion to disqualify.” *JJN FLB, LLC v. CFLB Partnership, LLC*, 283 So. 3d 922, 925 (Fla. 3d DCA 2019).

Petitioner challenges the October 8, 2026 order denying his verified motion for disqualification as legally insufficient. The order is included as Appendix A.

# V. GOVERNING LAW

## A. Rule 2.330 governs the motion

Fla. R. Gen. Prac. & Jud. Admin. 2.330 applies to county and circuit judges acting as the sole judicial officer in a trial or appellate proceeding. Subdivision (e)(1) provides that a motion may be based on the following ground:

> “The party reasonably fears that he or she will not receive a fair trial or hearing because of specifically described prejudice or bias of the judge.” Fla. R. Gen. Prac. & Jud. Admin. 2.330(e)(1).

Subdivision (h) limits the initial judge’s review:

> “The judge against whom an initial motion to disqualify under subdivision (e) is directed may determine only the legal sufficiency of the motion and shall not pass on the truth of the facts alleged.” Fla. R. Gen. Prac. & Jud. Admin. 2.330(h).

The rule further provides:

> “If any motion is legally insufficient, an order denying the motion shall immediately be entered. No other reason for denial shall be stated, and an order of denial shall not take issue with the motion.” Fla. R. Gen. Prac. & Jud. Admin. 2.330(h).

If the motion is legally sufficient:

> “The judge shall immediately enter an order granting disqualification and proceed no further in the action.” Fla. R. Gen. Prac. & Jud. Admin. 2.330(h).

## B. The objective standard

The Florida Supreme Court has held:

> “The question of disqualification focuses on those matters from which a litigant may reasonably question a judge’s impartiality rather than the judge’s perception of his ability to act fairly and impartially.” *Livingston v. State*, 441 So. 2d 1083, 1086–87 (Fla. 1983).

The question is whether the facts alleged, assumed to be true, would cause a reasonably prudent person to fear that he or she would not receive a fair and impartial hearing. *MacKenzie v. Super Kids Bargain Store, Inc.*, 565 So. 2d 1332, 1334–35 (Fla. 1990).

The judge deciding the initial motion may not determine the truth of the allegations, resolve factual disputes, or defend the challenged conduct. *Bundy v. Rudd*, 366 So. 2d 440, 441–42 (Fla. 1978).

The Florida Supreme Court has also recognized that disqualification protects the litigant’s right to appear before a judge whose neutrality is not reasonably questioned:

> “Every litigant, including the State in criminal cases, is entitled to nothing less than the cold neutrality of an impartial judge.” *Livingston v. State*, 441 So. 2d 1083, 1086 (Fla. 1983).

## C. Judicial-ethics provisions confirm the impartiality standard

The Florida Code of Judicial Conduct provides:

> “A judge shall disqualify himself or herself in a proceeding in which the judge’s impartiality might reasonably be questioned.” Fla. Code Jud. Conduct, Canon 3E(1).

The Code also provides that a judge shall perform judicial duties without bias or prejudice and shall be faithful to the law. Petitioner does not contend that the Code independently vacates any order. The Code is cited as confirming the objective impartiality principle embodied in Rule 2.330.

## D. Timeliness

Rule 2.330(g) provides:

> “A motion to disqualify shall be filed within a reasonable time not to exceed 20 days after discovery by the party or the party’s counsel, whichever is earlier, of the facts constituting the grounds for the motion.” Fla. R. Gen. Prac. & Jud. Admin. 2.330(g).

Petitioner recognizes that timeliness is a substantial issue in this case. The June 19 and July 17 events occurred more than twenty days before the October 6 motion. Petitioner does not contend that a later order automatically revives every earlier ground.

The October 2 order, however, supplied a distinct and materially different procedural fact: the court prohibited further discovery while requiring Petitioner to respond to a potentially dispositive standing issue, even though the written assignment and Wesley’s sworn affidavit had been filed months earlier.

Petitioner filed the motion four days after the October 2 order. To the extent the Court determines that the October 2 order constituted a newly discovered fact forming part of the asserted ground, the motion was filed within twenty days of that event.

Petitioner presents this argument candidly and does not ask the Court to accept an inaccurate discovery date. The petition challenges the denial on the basis that, assuming the October 2 event and the other alleged facts to be true, the motion was legally sufficient. Alternatively, Petitioner requests that the Court identify whether timeliness, rather than legal sufficiency, was the actual basis for denial.

# VI. ARGUMENT

## A. The petition properly presents a narrow challenge to legal sufficiency

The October 8 order expressly denied the motion as “legally insufficient.” This petition therefore asks this Court to review whether the alleged facts, assumed to be true, satisfied the objective standard under Rule 2.330.

Petitioner does not ask this Court to determine whether Judge Cohen’s underlying orders were legally correct. He does not seek review here of:

- the arbitration ruling;
- the June 19 scheduling order;
- the July 17 jurisdictional show-cause order;
- the October 2 discovery restriction;
- the standing issue;
- the assignability of any particular claim;
- or the merits of the underlying action.

Those issues may require separate motions, appeals, petitions, or other procedures. This petition concerns only whether the alleged procedural course created a legally sufficient fear of partiality.

## B. The motion alleged a cumulative procedural course, not merely adverse rulings

Petitioner recognizes that adverse judicial rulings, standing alone, ordinarily do not establish disqualification. The motion did not rely on one adverse ruling in isolation. It alleged a continuing sequence in which:

1\. The June 16 order left Elite Auto in the trial-court proceeding at that stage but permitted further briefing.

2\. The June 19 order immediately stayed the case as to Elite Auto and required threshold briefing before the case could proceed.

3\. Petitioner promptly sought clarification or vacatur of the stay.

4\. The July 17 order denied that request without a hearing and issued a jurisdictional show-cause order.

5\. The October 2 order again prohibited discovery while requiring briefing on standing.

6\. The October 2 order treated standing as potentially dispositive even though the assignment and affidavit had been filed months earlier.

7\. The cumulative effect was to prevent ordinary factual development of the Elite Auto claims while repeatedly making threshold issues prerequisites to proceeding.

The motion alleged that these facts caused Petitioner to fear that the Court had adopted a threshold posture that would prevent him from receiving a fair opportunity to develop and present his claims.

At the legal-sufficiency stage, the question was not whether the Court believed Petitioner’s interpretation of the orders. The question was whether the alleged sequence, taken as true, would cause a reasonably prudent litigant to fear that he would not receive a fair hearing.

## C. The October 2 order was a distinct, timely relevant event

The October 2 order is not merely a restatement of the June 19 order. The June 19 order stayed the case pending threshold briefing concerning Elite Auto. The October 2 order later prohibited “no further discovery” while requiring Petitioner to respond to Defendants’ standing arguments.

The October 2 order also stated that it did not appear that Petitioner had previously had an opportunity to address standing. Petitioner’s position is that this statement and the discovery prohibition materially altered the procedural posture of the case because:

- the written assignment and affidavit had already been filed;
- the assignment was directly relevant to the standing issue;
- the Court’s earlier orders had already restricted ordinary litigation;
- Defendants’ standing argument threatened dismissal of multiple claims; and
- discovery remained prohibited while the Court considered the threshold issue.

Petitioner filed his disqualification motion on October 6, four days after the October 2 order. The motion therefore alleged a distinct recent event that was timely when measured from October 2. Petitioner does not ask the Court to revive stale grounds merely by relabeling them. The earlier orders are included as context demonstrating the practical effect of the October 2 discovery prohibition and the cumulative procedural course.

## D. The allegations were required to be assumed true

The motion alleged that the June 19 stay took effect without a separately noticed motion or hearing directed to the scope and necessity of the stay. It alleged that Petitioner promptly sought clarification or vacatur, that his request was denied without a hearing, and that a jurisdictional show-cause order followed.

The motion did not ask Judge Cohen to admit that these allegations were true. It asked only that the Court determine their legal sufficiency. Under Rule 2.330(h), the Court could not decide whether:

- the June 19 order was authorized;
- a hearing was legally required;
- the stay was properly scoped;
- the July 17 order correctly analyzed jurisdiction;
- the October 2 order properly restricted discovery; or
- the assignment established standing.

Those are merits or procedural questions. The legal-sufficiency inquiry required the allegations to be accepted as true and viewed from the perspective of a reasonably prudent litigant.

## E. The October 8 denial did not identify whether timeliness or another defect was the basis for denial

Petitioner acknowledges that Rule 2.330(h) directs the judge not to state other reasons for denial and not to take issue with the motion. Petitioner does not contend that the brevity of the October 8 order alone requires disqualification.

The issue is that the order does not disclose whether the court rejected the motion because:

- it found the facts legally insufficient;
- it found the motion untimely;
- it rejected the October 2 order as a new ground;
- it concluded that the allegations involved only adverse rulings; or
- it relied on another procedural defect.

Because this Court reviews legal sufficiency de novo, it may independently determine whether the alleged facts satisfied Rule 2.330. *JJN FLB, LLC v. CFLB Partnership, LLC*, 283 So. 3d 922, 925 (Fla. 3d DCA 2019).

Petitioner respectfully requests that the Court not affirm on an unstated ground without permitting Petitioner to address that ground through the ordinary appellate process.

## F. The motion did not rely on the June 19 filing time or Juneteenth as independent proof of bias

Petitioner’s motion identified that the June 19 order was entered at approximately 6:12 p.m. on Juneteenth. Petitioner does not contend that the time of filing, the holiday, or after-hours entry independently establishes bias or requires disqualification.

Those facts were included as part of the chronology. The substantive allegation is that the order immediately stayed the Elite Auto case and imposed threshold proceedings before Petitioner had a meaningful opportunity to address the scope and necessity of the stay.

The petition therefore does not ask this Court to infer judicial bias merely from the order’s filing time.

## G. The assignment and affidavit are relevant to the objective fear alleged in the motion

The October 2 order prohibited discovery while treating standing as potentially dispositive. The record, however, included Wesley’s notarized affidavit and the written assignment identified by Petitioner.

The affidavit states that Wesley:

- owned the claims arising from the vehicle transaction;
- had not previously transferred or released them;
- assigned them to Petitioner for consideration;
- intended to transfer full ownership;
- retained no ownership, control, or right to proceeds;
- intended Petitioner to have the exclusive right to pursue and litigate the claims; and
- ratified the assignment and consented to joinder if required.

Petitioner does not ask this Court in this proceeding to decide whether every claim is assignable. The relevance of the assignment and affidavit is narrower. They support the allegation that the Court continued to treat standing as an unresolved barrier to discovery despite having before it sworn evidence directly addressing the issue.

A reasonably prudent litigant could view the repeated threshold barriers, coupled with the continuing discovery prohibition, as creating a fear that the claims would not receive ordinary and impartial adjudication. Whether that fear is ultimately correct is not for the initial judge to decide under Rule 2.330(h).

## H. The relief requested is prohibition, not appellate correction of the underlying orders

Petitioner recognizes that a writ of prohibition cannot be used as a substitute for an appeal from every unfavorable ruling. The relief requested here is limited. Petitioner asks this Court to:

1\. Review the October 8 order de novo;

2\. Determine whether the verified motion was legally sufficient when its factual allegations are assumed true;

3\. Quash the October 8 order if the motion was legally sufficient;

4\. Direct disqualification and reassignment under Rule 2.330; and

5\. Prohibit Judge Cohen from taking further substantive action in the underlying case, except as permitted by Rule 2.330.

Petitioner does not request that this Court vacate the June 16, June 19, July 17, or October 2 orders through this proceeding.

# VII. REQUEST FOR STAY
