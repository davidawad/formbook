# JURISDICTION AND VENUE

Section 120.68(1)(b), Fla. Stat. (2025) provides that “a preliminary, procedural, or intermediate order of … an administrative law judge of [DOAH] is immediately reviewable if review of the final agency action would not provide an adequate remedy.” The granting of a motion to disqualify counsel is such an order, justifying immediate review. *Gonzalez v. Chillura*, 892 So.2d 1075, 1076 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004) (Finding review by certiorari appropriate because, “The effect of an order granting such a motion is to deny a party counsel of its choice, a material injury without remedy on appeal.”).

Fla. R. App. P. 9.190(b)(2) provides that review of nonfinal agency action under the Administrative Procedures Act (APA), including nonfinal action by and administrative law judge, … must be commenced by filing a petition for review in accordance with Fla. R. App. P. 9.100(b) and (c)(3). Venue is appropriate in this court. Fla. R. App. P. 9.030(b) confers on district courts of appeal jurisdiction to review by appeal administrative action if provided by general law. Section 120.68(2), Fla. Stat. (2025) provides that judicial review of administrative actions under the APA shall be sought in the appellate district where the agency maintains its headquarters or where a party resides or as otherwise provided by law.”

# SCOPE OF REVIEW

The scope of review for judicial review of administrative action is provided by law in § 120.68(6), (7) and (8):

> (6)(a) The reviewing court’s decision may be mandatory, prohibitory, or declarative in form, and it shall provide whatever relief is appropriate, irrespective of the form of the petition. The court may:
>
> 1\. Order agency action required by law; … set aside agency action; … or decide the rights, privileges, obligations, requirements, or procedures at issue between the parties; and
>
> 2\. Order such ancillary relief as the court finds necessary to redress the effects of official action wrongfully taken or withheld.
>
> (7) The court shall remand a case to the agency for further proceedings consistent with the court’s decision or set aside agency action, as appropriate, when it finds that:
>
> (a) There has been no hearing prior to agency action and the reviewing court finds that the validity of the action depends on disputed facts;
>
> (b) The agency’s action depends on any finding of fact that is not supported by competent, substantial evidence in the record of a hearing conducted pursuant to ss. 120.569 and 120.57; however the court shall not substitute its judgment for that of an agency as to the weight of the evidence on any disputed issue of fact;
>
> (c) The fairness of the proceedings or the correctness of the action may have been impaired by a material error in procedure or a failure to follow prescribed procedure;
>
> (d) The agency has erroneously interpreted a provision of law and a correct interpretation compels a particular action; or
>
> (e) The agency’s exercise of discretion was:
>
> 1\. Outside the range of discretion delegated to the agency by law;
>
> 2\. Inconsistent with agency rule;
>
> 3\. Inconsistent with official stated agency policy or a prior agency practice, if deviation therefrom is not explained by the agency; or
>
> 4\. Otherwise in violation of a constitutional or statutory provision; but the court shall not substitute its judgment for that of the agency on an issue of discretion.
>
> (8) Unless the court finds a ground for setting aside, modifying remanding, or ordering agency action or ancillary relief under a specified provision of this section, it shall affirm the agency’s action.

See *BAM Trading Services, Inc. v. Office of Financial Regulation*, 395 So.3d 687 (Fla 1st DCA 2024), overruling *State v. Murciano*, 163 So.3d 662 (Fla 1st DCA 2015). In *Murciano*, the First District Court of Appeal defined the scope of review for a petition seeking review of non-final agency action under section 120.68(1) to be “analogous to, and no broader than the right of review by common law certiorari” and therefore to obtain relief a petitioner was required to demonstrate that the agency had departed from the essential requirements of the law. 163 So.2d at 664-665. In *BAM Trading Services*, The Court sitting *en banc* declined to apply that definition and “overrule[d] the erroneous holdings in *Murciano* … and any other decision where we held that our scope of review under section 120.68(7) ‘is analogous to and no broader than the right of review by common law certiorari.’” 395 So.3d at 689: “[N]either the constitution nor the Legislature equated our review to the demanding standard applicable to common law certiorari review. In addition, in section 120.60(6), the Legislature provided that such orders are subject to ‘judicial review.’” *Id.*

# STATEMENT OF FACTS

## A. Petitioner’s Petition for Termination

On April 21, 2025, Scott Hebert, as Superintendent of the School District of Citrus County, Florida, filed a Petition for Termination which was a statement of charges against the Respondent Teresa Royal in support of the Superintendent’s recommendation to The School Board of Citrus County, Florida that Royal’s employment with the School Board be terminated for just cause. (Apx. 134-146, Letter and Petition for termination).[^2] The Petition alleged that Royal violated Citrus County School Board (CCSB) Policy 6.26 which prohibits retaliation against an employee by another employee for asserting or alleging an act of bullying or harassment, and CCSB Policy 6.25 which makes applicable to all School Board employees the Principles of Professional Conduct for the Education Profession in Florida, specifically Fla. Admin. Code Rule 6A-10.081(2)(c)1, which requires individuals to maintain honesty in all professional dealings, Rule 6A-10.081(2)(c)4, which prohibits harassment or discriminatory conduct which unreasonably interferes with an individual’s performance of professional or work responsibilities, and 6A-10.081(2)(c)5, prohibiting malicious or intentionally false statements about a colleague.

The Petitioner has been represented at all times relevant to this matter by Thomas M. Gonzalez, Petitioner’s outside counsel for employment matters. On April 30, 2025, Royal, through her attorney Bobi Frank, requested a hearing on the Superintendent’s recommendation and the School Board referred the matter to the Division of Administrative Hearings (DOAH). The hearing was set for hearing on August 27 and 28, 2025.

## B. ROYAL REQUESTS GONZALEZ’S DEPOSITION

On July 15, 2025, Respondent served four requests for production of documents, none of which sought identification of persons having knowledge of the matters alleged in the petition for termination or the knowledge those persons would have or was otherwise addressed to the allegations of the Petition for Termination. (Apx. 147-152).[^3] On July 22, 2025, Respondent’s counsel’s office informed Petitioner that she needed to depose Gonzalez.

Gonzalez responded with the following:

> I don’t understand. Whether I am a “primary” witness isn’t the test. It’s whether I’m a necessary witness for my client. The rule itself doesn’t apply to a deposition of an opposing attorney. Whether you can do that depends on whether I have factual information that can’t be obtained [from another source]. What would that information be? I didn’t interview anyone for the investigation of Ms. Royal’s allegations of bullying. The only facts I had are the statements she volunteered to the investigator who investigated Ms. Royal’s claims of an unsafe working environment. I’ll be glad to talk but first I would ask for some written statement of the many questions that only I can answer. (Apx. 387, Attachment D)

Hearing nothing, Gonzalez reached out to opposing counsel on July 28, 2025:

> I haven’t gotten a response from my last email so I’m writing back to see if we can get this case moving. … With respect to taking my deposition, since you haven’t told me what you want to depose me about, we’re not going to agree to taking it. Opposing counsel’s response was to provide dates for the deposition, state her intention to issue a subpoena, and write:

I also want to know about your communications with CCSB and the like. Clearly only you can answer those questions. ### 1. The Rules of Professional Conduct do not Require Gonzalez’s Withdrawal or Prevent his Testimony on Undisputed Issues

R. Regulating Fla. Bar. 4-3.7 prohibits a member of The Florida Bar from acting as an advocate at a trial in which the lawyer is likely to be a necessary witness on behalf of the client unless the testimony will relate to an uncontested issue or the disqualification of the lawyer would work a substantial hardship on the client. By its express terms, Rule 4-3.7 applies to attorneys representing the client on whose behalf the attorney will testify. The Rule has been used to call opposing counsel as a witness but only upon a sufficient showing that the testimony cannot be obtained by other means and “only if the attorney’s testimony will be sufficiently adverse to the factual assertions or account of events offered [by the attorney] on behalf of the client.” *TMH Medical Services, LLC v. National Union Fire Insurance Company of Pittsburg, PA* 2018 WL 6620891, \*4 (U.S.D.C. M.D. Fla. 09/18/2018) (emphasis added) (citing *Lancaster v. Harrow*, 2018 WL 1274754, \*2-4 (U.S.D.C. M.D. Fla. 02/21/2018)).[^9] Despite having a deposition and a transcript of the first day of hearing, Respondent has not identified any testimony which meets that standard. More importantly, her claim of entitlement to call Gonzalez rests on her disregard for the proper standard that should have been applied.

Specifically, Respondent claims Gonzalez’s testimony will prejudice Petitioner. That is argument. The alleged acts or omissions that will be used to support that argument are Gonzalez’s. None involves a disputed issue of fact. None will be part of Petitioner’s case and no testimony Gonzalez could provide will contradict any part of Petitioner’s case. In the context of an attempt to compel a party’s attorney to testify on behalf of the opposing party, a narrow focus has been applied in assessing prejudice. *Cazares v. Church of Scientology of California, Inc.*, 429 So.2d 348, 350 (Fla. 5th DCA 1983). (“The mere assertion that the attorney’s testimony may prejudice his client has been held to be insufficient.”) (Id.).[^10] The burden on Respondent is a heavy one, as illustrated in *TMH Medical Services*:

> Given the disfavored relief requested-disqualification of one’s chosen attorney-the Court expects Defendant’s position as to the alleged adverse nature of [the plaintiff’s attorney’s] expected testimony to be clearly and unambiguously stated and sufficiently supported via record evidence. Further, Defendant’s attempt to divine what position TMH “must take” at trial is at odds with the position that TMH is taking at this time, as necessarily conceded by Defendant. …The undersigned expresses absolutely no opinion on the validity of TMH’s position…but simply finds that Defendant has not carried its burden to establish that [the attorney’s] potential testimony (at least as set forth in the record before the Court) is sufficiently adverse to TMH’s position in this case to require disqualification of [the attorney] as trial counsel. 2028 WL 6620891 at 4 (emphasis added)

*TMH Medical Services* cites to *Alto Construction Company, Inc.*, 22 So.3d at 728, in which the Court held:

> It follows that the circuit court departed from the essential requirements of law when it disqualified Alto’s attorney without determining whether [the attorney’s] testimony would be adverse to Alto’s factual assertions or accounts of events. Thus we grant the petition and quash the order disqualifying Ms. Jahn as Alto’s counsel. (emphasis added).

The *Alto* court cited to *AlliedSignal Recovery Trust*, 934 So.2d at 679 (quoting *Allstate Insurance Company v. English*, 588 So.2d 294, 295) (“An attorney that will be called as a witness by an opposing party may be disqualified if the attorney’s testimony will ‘be sufficiently adverse to the factual assertions or accounts of events offered on behalf of on behalf of the client.’”) (emphasis added).

Respondent argues that it is “obvious that the testimony to be elicited from Mr. Gonzalez will be adverse to the interests of the client he represents,” because, “[t]o the extent Petitioner intends to stand behind the decision to terminate Respondent, Gonzalez’s testimony will be used to show that the process was inherently flawed and violative of both School Board practices and fundamental due process.” (Apx. 298-299, Motion, pp. 7-8). These alleged violations are described in the Motion. There has been no decision to terminate and while Respondent may argue that the process by which Gonzalez conducted his investigation of Royal’s complaint of bullying and harassment is relevant to the question of where just cause exists for Respondent’s termination, Gonzalez will provide no testimony that would contradict any fact the Petitioner will put forth on that question.

Respondent supports his assertion of Gonzalez’s toxicity to Petitioner’s case with contentions first made in her motion seeking disqualification. Gonzalez conducted his investigation of Royal’s complaints of bullying without interviewing a single witness “despite the fact that the subject of potential discipline and known witnesses are *always* interviewed during the course of School board investigations” (Id. pp. 3-4, emphasis in the original), Gonzalez “‘second guessed’ … findings of fact and credibility assessments made in a prior, related investigation and report,” (Id. p. 4), Gonzalez never debriefed the . . . the Superintendent on any aspect of his investigation or his recommendation of termination (Id. 5), Gonzalez issued the termination letter “which gave effect to his report and recommended discipline and also filed the Petition for Termination” (Id. p. 6), “[a]s the sole investigator, Gonzalez is the only person who can testify as to the reasons why termination is appropriate (Id.), Gonzalez demonstrated bias that “goes beyond zealous representation of a client” by and “the multiple roles assumed by Mr. Gonzalez as investigator, *de facto* *decisionmaker* and prosecutor clearly prejudice the Respondent. (Id. 8).[^11]

Disqualification of a party’s chosen counsel is an extraordinary remedy that should be resorted to sparingly. *Arcara v. Phillip M. Warren, P.A.*, 574 So.2d 325 (Fla. 4th DCA 1991) (citations omitted). (The moving party bears the burden of demonstrating “the necessity of the attorney’s testimony and thus his disqualification”), citing *Hiatt v. Estate of Hiatt*, 837 So.2d 1132, 1133 (Fla. 4th DCA 2003).[^12] It is therefore worth noting that until ordered to do so by the Administrative Law Judge, Respondent never sought disqualification much less made any attempt to meet the standard applicable to the disqualification of opposing counsel on the grounds that he is an essential witness to both her defense and Petitioner’s case-in-chief.[^13] *Hiatt* clearly demonstrates its recognition of applicable law:

> Under the circumstances here, where no evidence was proffered or offered below, the [movant] has failed to carry her burden. Nothing in the record demonstrated that [opposing counsel] “is likely to be a necessary witness on behalf of his…client on a contested matter.” *Allstate Ins. Co. v. English*, 588 So.2d 294, 295 (Fla. 2d DCA 1991).

> Absent the [movant’s] showing that [the attorney] will be a necessary witness or that his testimony would be adverse to [his client’s] position, *see id.*, the court departed from the essential requirements of the law in granting the motion to discharge counsel. See *In re Estate of Gory*, 1381, 1383 (Fla. 4th DCA 1990); *Ray v. Stuckey*, 491 So.2d 1211, 1214. (emphasis added).

Respondent attempts to abbreviate the showing she is required to make after taking no discovery, filing no motion and refusing to divulge the testimony he deems essential to both his defense and the Respondent’s case but arguing that opposing counsel “will be a central figure” in the case. The case cited for that proposition is *Fleitman v. McPherson*, 691 So.2d 37 (Fla. 1st DCA 1997). The party seeking disqualification in that case, a plaintiff suing for defamation, was successful. The attorney whose disqualification he sought represented the defendant and would present evidence on behalf of his client about the conduct of the plaintiff that had formed the basis of the allegedly defamatory complaint the defendant had submitted to The Florida Bar. The court found, appropriately, that because his testimony may become a feature of the trial, he could not act at the defendant’s attorney at trial. But the “central figure” theory does not apply in this case and it would be a different case if the Plaintiff had sought to disqualify Defendant’s counsel because he was essential to Plaintiff’s case.[^14] In that situation, the party seeking disqualification would have to show more than the lawyer’s possession of evidence. Plaintiff would be required to show that the evidence the attorney would provide would be prejudicial to the facts or sequence of events that his client would put forth.

*Fleitman* cites to *Ray v. Stuckey*, 491 So.2d 1211, 1214 (Fla. 1st DCA 1986). *Stuckey* is a case in which a party sought to disqualify the other party’s attorney. The Court described the relevant to be applied:

> The correctness of an order involving the disqualification of an order involving the disqualification of counsel must be determined by testing it against the standards imposed by the Disciplinary Rules of the Code of Professional Responsibility. [citing *Cazares*, supra] . . .
>
> The concern of this rule is that trial counsel might be inhibited from attacking his own credibility or arguing to the fact-finder the lack of credibility of his testimony, thus affecting his ability to properly represent his client. The testimony is therefore prejudicial only when sufficiently adverse to the factual assertions or account of events offered on behalf of the client. (emphasis added).

The question is not whether Respondent can make opposing counsel a “central figure” in the case she wants to present. It is whether that lawyer would give testimony on a contested matter that differed from the facts or account the lawyer will present on behalf of his client. Respondent has yet to identify much less establish any testimony that Petitioner’s attorney could give that is adverse to the factual assertions or account of events offered on behalf of the Petition for Termination. In fact, the factual assertions or account of events that will be presented on behalf of the Petitioner are those set out in the Petition for Termination. Gonzalez has no first hand knowledge of those facts as he did not witness or create them. He cannot provide testimony that contradicts any of them.

Gonzalez’s role as an investigator does not require disqualification. In *AlliedSignal Recovery Trust v. AlliedSignal, Inc.*, *supra*, the court quashed an order of disqualification. “The motion stressed [the trial counsel’s] long relationship with [the plaintiff in a fraud suit brought against AlliedSignal], the fraud claims were based on [the attorney’s] investigation, and the advice he gave … created a conflict.” (*Allied Recovery Trust*, 934 So.2d at 677. In denying that motion, the Court said:

> [The lawyer subject of the motion] was not going to testify for his own client. … But because [the defendant] could call [the lawyer] to testify against his client, the trial court concluded that any conflict waiver … was unreasonable because “[a] disinterested lawyer could not take the chance” that a jury could react negatively to [the lawyer’s] extensive involvement with [the defendant]. [Petitioner] made no showing that the lawyer would give any testimony “sufficiently adverse to the factual allegations or account of events offered on behalf of the [client].” To allow disqualification without such a showing invites mischief. (934 So.2d at 679-70) (emphasis added).

The factual allegations and accounts that will be offered by Petitioner are those pled in the Petition for Termination. Respondent has not shown any testimony Gonzalez would give that would be adverse to those allegations and accounts.

### 2. Respondent’s Due Process Rights do not Include the Right to be Interviewed or to have Witnesses Interviewed

According to Respondent, “[t]he essential mystery in this case is how Ms. Royal came to be terminated in the absence of a formal sustained complaint.” (Apx. 293, p. 2). According to her, “[t]he evidence will show that the subject of potential discipline and known witnesses are always interviewed during the course of School Board investigations.” (Id. 4). One thing is certain and that is that Gonzalez cannot provide testimony that would support the latter assertion and the issue will be an element of Petitioner’s case in chief.

Respondent has no due process right to have an investigation before her discharge is recommended. She has cited no authority for the proposition that she is entitled to be interviewed or entitled to have “all” or any number of witnesses interviewed. The seminal case on pre-termination due process is *Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill*, 470 U.S. 532 (S.C. 1985) where the Supreme Court defined the process due a public employee possessed of a property interest in employment to be a pre-termination opportunity to respond, coupled with a post-termination evidentiary hearing. Those guarantees do not include an investigation before termination is recommended. In *Loudermill*, the employee was hired as a security guard after indicating on his application that he had never been convicted of a felony. Eleven months later, as part of a routine examination of his employment records, the board discovered that in fact he had been convicted of a felony. 470 U.S. at 535. The employee was informed by letter that his employment was terminated. The court found no fault with the absence of an investigation. It focused on what happened after the employer decided to pursue termination.

*Loudermill* instructs that the process owed the employee includes notice of the nature of the charges and an informal opportunity to deny, explain, or mitigate them. Due process does not include the right to an investigation before those charges are brought. That is not to say that Respondent is precluded in any way from arguing the contrary but that argument would not involve disputed issues of fact. Gonzalez did not interview witnesses. He did credit the summary of the unsworn answers the five persons who Royal complained against her over her own unsworn writings. His consideration of those words were the basis his recommendation that Royal had violated School Board policy. The Superintendent’s charges based on those recommendation will be proven not by Gonzalez’s or anyone else’s recommendation but by the witnesses presented in the hearing.

### 3. The Direction to a Party That It Should Consider Obtaining Another Attorney Violates Petitioner’s Rights to Its Choice of Counsel

After denying the motion to disqualify Gonzalez, the ALJ allowed Respondent to call Gonzalez as a witness, without any explanation of the basis for either action. Then the ALJ added the following statement: “In that regard, Petitioner should consider obtaining an attorney other than Gonzalez to represent [the School Board] for the remainder of this case. The attorney may be from Petitioner’s current attorney’s law firm.”[^15] “The law in Florida is clear—a judge may not enter into the fray by giving ‘tips’ or legal advice to either side.” *State v. Dixon*, 217 So.3d 1115, 1126 (Fla. 3d DCA 2017). A judge’s violation of that neutrality is in fact grounds for disqualification.

*Dixon* is cited in *State v. Adams*, 414 So.3d 375 (Fla. 3d DCA 2025) where the trial court was found to have erred by prompting an unrepresented party to make an untimely and insufficient *ore tenus* motion. 414 So.3d at 380 (“Here, it is clear that Adams would not have moved to dismiss . . . without prompting from the judge over the State’s objection.”). Here, Respondent represented that she did not seek Gonzalez’s disqualification. (Apx. 251, 9:17-24) (“We are not asking that Mr. Gonzalez be disqualified - - he probably should be, but the reason we’re not making that formal motion is purely self-interest[.]”). Respondent did press his intention to call Gonzalez as a witness and suggest that he should be disqualified. She did not identify any testimony to be elicited from Gonzalez that involved a disputed issue of fact and has not done so in the mandated motion to disqualify.

The ALJ announced that the hearing could not continue and asked if anything could be done “in the interim.” (Apx. 258-259, 16:18-17:23). Gonzalez responded: “If you’re saying you need me to be a witness, I can do that.” The ALJ asked if that would disqualify Gonzalez as counsel and Gonzalez responded that he did not believe it would. (Apx. 259-260, 17:24-18-4). Respondent objected. Gonzalez offered to allow Respondent to make a proffer “of what they want to get from me as a witness”. (Apx. 272, 30:13-22). Respondent declined the offer. The ALJ then said, “You need to offer up a corporate representative that can be questioned as to the *decision* and if you’re saying it’s just someone that signed off on *your decision*, that’s the problem. I can’t make findings of fact based on the lawyer’s determination if indeed you’re representing them and can’t be a witness.” (Apx. 273, 31:11-18) (emphasis added). Gonzalez responded as follows:

> I wouldn’t ask you to, Judge. I would ask you to make findings of fact based on the testimony of the witnesses who are going to testify to the untruthfulness and there’s going to be a credibility determination that you’re going to have to make, but those witnesses are going to testify. That’s who’s going to prove the lie. (Apx. 273, 31:18-24).

Even after Respondent acknowledged that he had not deposed any of the persons specifically identified in the petition for termination as a matter of “attorney strategy” and Gonzalez represented that he was prepared to proceed, the ALJ ordered Respondent to file a motion to disqualify Gonzalez and to “have him as a witness.” (Apx. 275, 33:12-22). The former, which was both untimely and without merit, was nominally denied; the latter granted but with the advice, spoken directly to the party, that it should consider changing counsel for the “remainder of the case.”[^16] The effect of that statement effectively disqualified Gonzalez.

If Gonzalez had an obligation to withdraw because he was a necessary witness on behalf of his client, that obligation rested on him, not the client. If Gonzalez could provide testimony contrary to the Petitioner’s case, Gonzalez should have been disqualified. The ALJ clearly took sides, by first requiring Respondent to file an untimely and meritless motion for disqualification and then advising the Petitioner to disregard its counsel’s opinion that he could testify to undisputed facts and instead retain substitute counsel for the “remainder” of the case. He did not advise an unrepresented party to get a lawyer. He told the Petitioner to get a different lawyer than the one it wanted. The Order for which review is sought is in fact a disqualification as well as an implicit finding that Respondent’s assertions of Gonzalez’s decision-making role in the termination are well-founded and his actions potentially unethical.

### 4. The Relief Sought by Petitioner

To allow Petitioner to have his counsel of their choice, this Court should grant review of the ALJ’s order, require him to rescind his order allowing Respondent to call Gonzalez as a witness and directing Petitioner to consider seeking new counsel for the remainder of the case and prohibit Respondent from calling Gonzalez as a witness in her behalf.

# CONCLUSION:

Respondent’s defense to charges that she filed false charges of bullying and harassment against five of six employees who had filed charges against her is that because no investigation was conducted before her termination, she must be absolved of wrongdoing, even if the Petitioner proves its charges. No law would compel such an outcome. Respondent nevertheless argues that theory. She may not, however, deprive Petitioner of counsel of its choice when there are no grounds for doing so.
