Pursuant to Section 120.68(1), Fla. Expedited review of these non-final orders in Division of Administrative Hearings (DOAH) Case No. 10-2988GM is necessary because review of the final agency decision after completion of the proceeding below would not provide an adequate remedy.

FWF contends the July 12th order dismissing the Florida Department of Community Affairs (DCA) as a party respondent unconstitutionally applied Chapter 2011-139, Laws of Florida retroactively, thereby denying FWF it’s vested rights and procedural rights to have the DCA as a party respondent.[^1]

FWF contends the August 1st notice that states the new Chapter 2011-139 is the governing law in the pending Section 163.3184(16)(f), Fla. Stat. below is unconstitutional for the multiple reasons set forth below.

# I. ALJ CANTER’S TWO NON-FINAL ORDERS

## A. The July 12th Order

The July 12th order granted the Florida Department of Community Affairs (DCA’s) motion to be dismissed as a party from the pending Section 163.3184(16)(f), Fla. Stat. proceeding below that is challenging DCA’s December 12, 2010 Cumulative Notice of Intent (NOI) that proposes to find Lee County’s comprehensive land use plan amendments in compliance with Chapter 163, Part II, Fla. Stat. (2010). This DCA Cumulative NOI is proposed DCA agency action for which FWF contends it has a vested right to have DCA defend as a respondent in the proceeding below.

The July 12th order further held that after dismissal of DCA the remaining parties in the proceeding below may call DCA employees as witnesses in the proceeding below to explain DCA’s action on the plan amendments under Chapter 163, Part II, Fla. Stat. (2010),[^2] and that the “issues, burden of proof, compliance criteria, and any other substantive matter” in the proceeding below will be those set forth in the old law, Chapter 163, Part II, Fla. Stat. (2010).[^3]

## B. The August 1st Notice

The August 1st notice sua sponte declared and ordered that the “issues raised in this case shall be governed by chapter 2011-139” and “the application of the new law is not a retroactive application of the law.” (App. 2). This sua sponte order is inconsistent with the July 12th order which held that the “issues, burden of proof, compliance criteria, and any other substantive matter” in the proceeding below will be those set forth in the old law, Chapter 163, Part II, Fla. Stat. (2010).

The August 1st notice imposes all of the procedural and substantive provisions of Chapter 2011-139 into the pending Section 163.3184(16)(f) proceeding below, thereby placing FWF’s claims that Chapter 2011-139 was unconstitutionally adopted in violation of the following mandates of the Florida Constitution at issue.

### 1. Declare (HB 7207) Chapter 2011-139 to Have Been Adopted In Violation of Art. III, Sections 6 & 7, Fla. Const.

The August 1st notice should be reversed because HB 7207 (Chapter 2011-139, Laws of Florida) unconstitutionally violates both the single subject rule of Art. III, Section 6, and the “passage of bills” mandate of Art. III, Section 7, Fla. Const.

Art. III, Section 7, of the Fla. Const. reads as follows.

> “Any bill may originate in either house and after passage in one may be amended in the other. It shall be read in each house on three separate days, unless this rule is waived by two-thirds vote; provided the publication of its title in the journal of a house shall satisfy the requirement for the first reading in that house. On each reading, it shall be read by title only, unless one-third of the members present desire it read in full. On final passage, the vote of each member voting shall be entered on the journal. Passage of a bill shall require a majority vote in each house. Each bill and joint resolution passed in both houses shall be signed by the presiding officers of the respective houses and by the secretary of the senate and the clerk of the house of representatives during the session or as soon as practicable after its adjournment sine die.”

The Florida Senate has interpreted and implemented the passage of bill requirements of Art. III, Section 7 of the Fla. Const. regarding what constitutes an amendment of a bill originating in the Florida House of Representatives, by adopting Senate Rule 2.41 which states that:

> “[a] proposal to delete everything after the enacting clause of a bill, or the resolving clause of a resolution, and insert new language of the same general subject matter as stated in the original title shall be deemed proper and germane and shall be treated as an amendment.”

In order for action that deletes everything after the enacting clause of a bill to be treated as an amendment, Senate Rule 2.41 requires the insertion of replacement text after the enacting clause. This Senate Rule 2.41 requirement is a reasonable and proper interpretation of what constitutes amendment of a bill originating in the other house in the Florida Legislature. The court should adopt this interpretation as what the first sentence in Art. III, Section 7 of the Fla. Const. means for an amendment of a bill that originated in the other house in the Florida Legislature. Otherwise, the passage of bills reading requirement for notice to the members of Legislature would be rendered meaningless.

HB 7207 was initially passed by the Florida House of Representatives on April 7, 2011 and was properly entitled “trust finds.” The Senate never passed or amended HB 7207 concerning trust funds. Rather, the Senate struck everything but the enabling clause, and no additional language was added to the bill. An enabling clause without a title or any text is not an amendment or even a bill. This Senate action was rejection of HB 7207 in its entirety in accordance with the mandates of Art III, Section 7, Fla. Stat. for passage of bills. It was a violation of Art. III, Section 7, Fla. Const. when the Senate inserted growth management text into the HB 7207 shell of a bill at the end of the 2011 Legislative session.

Chapter 2011-139 (HB 7207) is unconstitutional because it contains more than one subject and was adopted in violation of the single subject rule of Article III, Section 7 of the Fla. Const.

Article III, Section 6 of the Florida Constitution provides, in pertinent part, that “[e]very law shall embrace but one subject and matter properly connected therewith, and the subject shall be briefly expressed in the title.” This portion of the Florida Constitution is referred to as “the single subject rule.”

In Martinez v. Scanlan, 582 So.2d 1167 at 1172 (Fla.1991), the Florida Supreme Court stated:

> “The purpose of this constitutional prohibition against a plurality of subjects in a single legislative act is to prevent “logrolling” where a single enactment becomes a cloak for dissimilar legislation having no necessary or appropriate connection with the subject matter. The act may be as broad as the legislature chooses provided the matters included in the act have a natural or logical connection.”

The last title of HB 7207 related to “growth management,” a title that encompasses more than a single legislative subject, as illustrated by the appellate court decision in State v. Leavins, 599 So.2d 1326 (Fla. 1st DCA 1992), which held that an statute read and enacted under the title “an act relating to environmental resources” violated the single subject rule because of the range of topics addressed in the forty-eight sections of the bill.

> “This phrase [“an act relating to environmental resources”] is so broad, and potentially encompasses so many topics, that it lends little support to the State’s attempt to fend off a single subject challenge. . . Although each individual subject addressed [in chapter 89-175] might be said to bear some relationship to the general topic of environmental resources, such a finding would not, and should not, satisfy the test under Article III, Section 6. If a purpose of the constitutional prohibition [is] to insure, as nearly as possible, that a member of the legislature be able to consider the merit of each subject contained in the act independently of the political influence of the merit of each other topic, the reviewing court must examine each subject in light of the various other matters affected by the act, and not simply compare each isolated subject to the stated topic of the act. (e.s.). Leavin, Supra at 1172.”

HB 7207, last entitled “relating to growth management,”[^9] addressed more than one subject and is so broad and encompasses too many topics for a “member of the legislature be able to consider the merit of each subject contained in the act independently of the political influence of the merit of each other topic” even if properly noticed and titled. ### 2. Declare Chapter 2011-139 an Unlawful Delegation of Legislative Authority in violation of Art. II, Section 3

The August 1st Notice in the proceeding below puts at issue the constitutionality of Chapter 2011-139.[^11] The FWF contends Chapter 2011-139 unlawfully delegates legislative authority to DCA the duty to implement the unconstitutionally vague phrases: “important state resources and facilities,” “regionally significant resources,” “regionally significant water resources and wildlife corridors,” and “regional resources and facilities.” Chapter 2011-139 not only unlawful delegates legislative authority to DCA, Chapter 2011-139 also repeals DCA’s specific rule making authority in Section 163.3184(9) & (10), Fla. Stat. (2010),[^12] and it enacted new Section 163.3184(4), Fla. Stat (2011) that prohibits DCA from adopting rules to implement Chapter 2011-139. These actions eliminated DCA’s rule making authority, a situation that is further made illegal by existing Section 120.57(1)(e)(1), Fla. Stat. (2011) that prohibits agencies and ALJs from basing decisions on unadopted rules. Expedited review is needed to determine whether Chapter 2011-139 unlawfully delegates vague legislative authority.

Article II, Section 3 of the Florida Constitution, the non-delegation doctrine, reads as follows states:

> “Section 3. Branches of Government.– The powers of the state government shall be divided into legislative, executive and judicial branches. No person belonging to one branch shall exercise any powers appertaining to either of the other branches unless expressly provided herein.”

In the case of Florida Department of State, Division of Elections v. Martin, 916 So.2d 763, 769 (Fla. 2005), the Florida Supreme Court discusses the unlawful delegation of legislative law in Florida.

> “This Court has traditionally applied a “strict separation of powers doctrine,” State v. Cotton, 769 So.2d 345, 353 (Fla.2000), which “encompasses two fundamental prohibitions.” Chiles v. Children A, B, C, D, E, & F, 589 So.2d 260, 264 (Fla.1991). “The first is that no branch may encroach upon the powers of another. The second is that no branch may delegate to another branch its constitutionally assigned power.” (citation omitted). In Bush v. Schiavo, 885 So.2d 321 (Fla.2004), cert. denied, \_\_\_ U.S. \_\_\_, 125 S.Ct. 1086, 160 L.Ed.2d 1069 (2005), we recently addressed this second prohibition and explained:

> The Legislature is permitted to transfer subordinate functions “to permit administration of legislative policy by an agency with the expertise and flexibility to deal with complex and fluid conditions.” Microtel, Inc. v. Fla. Public Serv. Comm’n, 464 So.2d 1189, 1191 (Fla.1985). However, under article II, section 3 of the constitution the Legislature “may not delegate the power to enact a law or the right to exercise unrestricted discretion in applying the law.” Sims v. State, 754 So.2d 657, 668 (Fla.2000). This prohibition, known as the non-delegation doctrine, requires that “fundamental and primary policy decisions . . . be made by members of the legislature who are elected to perform those tasks, and [that the] administration of legislative programs must be pursuant to some minimal standards and guidelines ascertainable by reference to the enactment establishing the program.” Askew v. Cross Key Waterways, 372 So.2d 913,925 (Fla.1978)\.\.\. In other words, statutes granting power to the executive branch “must clearly announce adequate standards to guide . . . in the execution of the powers delegated. The statute must so clearly define the power delegated that the [executive] is precluded from acting through whim, showing favoritism, or exercising unbridled discretion.” Lewis v. Bank of Pasco County, 346 So.2d 53, 55-56 (Fla.1976)\.\.\..

> The requirement that the Legislature delineate adequate standards enables courts to perform their constitutional duties. The failure to set forth adequate standards precludes a court from determining whether the executive branch is acting in accord with the Legislature’s intent. See, Askew, 372 So.2d at 918-19 (“When legislation is so lacking in guidelines that neither the agency nor the courts can determine whether the agency is carrying out the intent of the legislature in its conduct, then, in fact, the agency becomes the lawgiver rather than the administrator of the law.”).”

The Florida Supreme Court has in several decisions ruled unconstitutional laws that delegated legislative authority with insufficient standards guiding the exercise of this authority by the executive branch. In Lewis, the Florida Supreme Court held that a statute allowing bank or trust company records to be made public upon the consent of the Comptroller, as head of the Department of Banking and Finance, constituted an unconstitutional delegation of legislative authority in violation of Article II, Section 3. Lewis, Supra at 55. In holding Section 658.10(1) unconstitutional, the Court approved the reasoning of the circuit court judge who found that “[t]here are no restrictions, limitations, or guidelines provided in the statute to limit or regulate the action of the department in granting . . . [or] withholding consent.” Id. at 55.

In Orr v. Trask, 464 So.2d 131 (Fla.1985), the Court concluded that a proviso authorizing the Governor to reduce the number of deputy commissioner positions violated Article II, Section 3. The Court determined that the proviso was unconstitutional because it “furnished no guidance to the Governor as to the criteria to be used in reducing the number of deputies,” but rather, left the selection of which deputies should be eliminated “entirely to the unbridled discretion of the executive branch.” Id. at 134.

In Bush v. Schiavo, 885 So.2d 321 (Fla.2004), the Florida Supreme Court held that Chapter 2003-418, Laws of Florida, was an unconstitutional delegation of legislative authority in violation of Article II, Section 3. See, Schivo, 885 So.2d at 336. In holding Chapter 2003-418 was unconstitutional, the Court explained that chapter 2003-418 failed to provide any standards by which the Governor could decide whether to issue a stay and how long a stay should remain in effect. See id., at 334. The Court stated that “[t]his absolute, unfettered discretion to decide whether to issue and when to lift a stay makes the Governor’s decision virtually unreviewable.” Id.

HB 7207 uses the vague and undefined phrases “important state resources and facilities,” “important regional resources and facilities,” “regionally significant natural resources,” “regionally significant water courses and wildlife corridors,” and “regionally resources and facilities” in the following fundamental portions of the new act.

A. The phrase “important state resources and facilities” is used in the new Section 163.3161(3), Fla. Stat., namely the intent to “focus the state role in managing growth under this act to protecting the functions of important state resources and functions.”[^13]

B. The new Section 163.3164(3), Fla. Stat. directs DCA “to provide direct and indirect technical assistance to local governments if plan amendments may adversely affect important state resources or facilities.” Without more statutory definition this language cannot legally be implemented by DCA.

C. The new Section 163.3184(3), Fla. Stat. provides that state agencies may only comment upon “important state resources and facilities” when commenting upon plan amendments. FWF contends this statutory limitation on state agency comments without defining the phrase “important state resources and facilities,” especially with the Chapter 2011-139 prohibition on the state land planning agency adopting rules defining the phrases, makes this delegation of legislative authority unconstitutional in violation of Art. II, Section 3, and Art. II, Section 7 of the Fla. Const.

D. The new Section 163.3184(4), Fla. Stat. which limits the state land planning agency to objections, recommendations, and comments (ORC) in its report regarding a plan amendment only if the plan amendment “will adversely affect important state resources and facilities.” This vague undefined phrase prohibits the state land planning agency from commenting in its ORC on plan amendment which improve and provide needed new protection to important state resources and facilities as do the Lee County plan amendments in the pending proceeding below.

E. The new Section 163.3184(5)(b), Fla. Stat. which allows the state land planning agency to file a petition with DOAH only after a state land planning agency determination that an “important state resource or facility” will be adversely affected by the adopted plan amendment.

F. The new Section 163.3184(5)(c)(3), Fla. Stat. attempts to establish a new standard of proof in DCA initiated proceedings involving “important state resources and facilities.” However, without more statutory definition of the phrase, the standard of proof cannot be implemented by ALJs because Section 120.57(1)(e)(1), Fla. Stat. prohibits reliance on unadopted rules.

G. The new Section 163.3245, Fla. Stat. concerning “Optional Sector plans” uses the vague and undefined phrases “regionally significant resources,” regionally significant water courses and wildlife corridors,” and “regionally regional resources and facilities” which sector plans must protect. Without definition of these phrases sector plans cannot be implemented.

H. The new Section 163.3245(3)(a), Fla. Stat. concerning the long term master plans of sector plans uses the phrases “regionally significant natural resources” and “other important resources both within and outside the host jurisdiction” as what needs to be identified and protected by specific measures through recorded conservation easements. Without definitions of these phrases the identification and protection activities can not be performed.

Application of Chapter 2011-139 to the proceeding below places the proceeding below in gridlock. DCA can not decide whether to provide Lee County with technical assistance, and DCA and other state agencies can not perform the reviews required by the new Chapter 163, Part II, Fla. Stat.

### 3. Declare Chapter 2011-139 Unconstitutionally Results In Inadequate Laws To Conserve and Protect Florida’s Natural Resources as required by Art. II, Section 7, of the Fla. Const.

HB 7207 unconstitutionally delegates vague legislative authority to the DCA concerning “important state resources and facilities,” and “important regional resources and facilities” in violation of Article II, Section 3 of the Fla. Stat. HB 7207 not only does not define these terms, and HB 7207 also prohibits DCA from having the statutory authority to adopt rules to implement these phrases. In light of the prohibition in Section 120.57(1)(e)(1), Fla. Stat on agencies and ALJs determining substantial interests of parties based upon unadopted rules, HB 7207 unconstitutionally violates Art. II, Section 7 of the Fla. Const. which mandates that “[a]dequate provision shall be made by law for the abatement of air and water pollution\.\.\..and for the conservation and protection of natural resources.”

At a minimum state statutes must provide adequate provision to abate air and water and to conserve and protect the natural resources of the State of Florida by enacting adequate comprehensive land use laws.[^14] The Chapter 163, Part II, Fla. Stat. Growth Management Act laws revised by Chapter 2011-139 are not adequate because they do not define “important state resources and facilities” or “regionally significant natural resources,” and “regionally significant water courses and wildlife corridors.” Without definitions and rule making authority to implement Chapter 2011-139, there are not adequate provision by law to abate air and water and to conserve and protect the natural resources of the State of Florida as mandated by Art. II, Section, 7, Fla. Const.
