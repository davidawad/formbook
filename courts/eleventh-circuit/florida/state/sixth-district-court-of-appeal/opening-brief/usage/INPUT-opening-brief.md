# STATEMENT OF PRESERVATION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW

Each issue presented in this appeal was properly preserved in the lower tribunal through written responses in opposition to summary judgment, (R.144–176) a motion for reconsideration, (R.631- 784) response in opposition to partial summary judgment (R.310-342) and oral arguments presented during hearings (R.1161-1262).

The standard of review for this Court is de novo over a trial court's order granting summary judgment. *Palm Holdings, Ltd. v. Riverfront Plaza, LLC*, 379 So.3d 547, 550 (Fla. 6th DCA 2024) (quoting *Volusia County v. Aberdeen at Ormond Beach, L.P.*, 760 So. 2d 126, 130 (Fla. 2000)). Summary judgment is proper "if the movant shows that there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.510(a). The moving party bears the initial burden of demonstrating the absence of a genuine dispute of material fact. The non-moving party must then identify record evidence demonstrating that "a reasonable jury could find in its favor." *Shiver v. Chertoff*, 549 F.3d 1342, 1343 (11th Cir. 2008); *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 324–25 (1986). In reviewing a summary-judgment ruling, the Court must view the evidence "in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and draw all reasonable inferences in that party's favor." *Newcomb v. Spring Creek Cooler, Inc.*, 926 F.3d 709, 713 (11th Cir. 2019). The moving party may carry its burden by "demonstrating an absence of evidence supporting the nonmoving party's case"; the burden then shifts to the nonmoving party to point to evidence in the record "establishing a genuine issue for trial." *Celotex*, 477 U.S. at 324–25.

# SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

Summary judgment was improper under Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.510 because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding the very existence and applicability of an enforceable contract governing the Orlando project. D.R. Horton relied on a contract facially restricted to its Tampa division for a home constructed in Orlando, and the attached instrument was incomplete. Furthermore, because the contract identity is disputed, D.R. Horton failed to establish compliance with the monetary limitation and commercial relationship requirements of Florida Statute §725.06(1).

D.R. Horton's designated Corporate Representative admitted under oath during a Rule 1.310(b)(6) deposition that the corporation could neither produce nor confirm an Orlando division contract nor verify that the Tampa agreement applied. Under Florida law, these sworn corporate admissions are binding and cannot be contradicted or nullified by self-serving pre-deposition affidavits to manufacture summary judgment.

The underlying claims in the lawsuit against D.R. Horton were strictly rooted in D.R. Horton's exclusive product selection ("C" Channel windows), rather than any installation error, workmanship defect, or manufacturing flaw attributable to ProBuild. Because Ramjit voluntarily dismissed Count IV, the sole window-type selection claim before D.R. Horton answered, and D.R. Horton dropped its indemnity claims, no claim triggered ProBuild's duty to defend as a matter of law.

# ARGUMENT

## The Lower Tribunal Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Where Genuine Issues of Material Fact Persist Regarding the Existence, Applicability, and Statutory Compliance of the Operative Contract Under Fla. Stat. §725.06.

The trial court committed reversible error in granting summary judgment in favor of D.R. Horton because genuine issues of material fact remain unresolved regarding whether the Florida Independent Contractor Agreement ever legally governed the Orlando construction project at issue, whether the agreement complies with mandatory statutory prerequisites under Fla. Stat. § 725.06(1), and how responsibility for product selection was allocated between the parties.

Appellate review of an order granting summary judgment is conducted de novo. *Volusia Cnty. v. Aberdeen at Ormond Beach, L.P.*, 760 So.2d 126, 130 (Fla. 2000). Under Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.510(a), summary judgment is appropriate only when "the movant shows that there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.510(a). Florida adheres to the federal summary judgment standard and the Celotex trilogy, which mandates that the moving party affirmatively demonstrate the complete absence of any genuine factual dispute. *In re Amends. to Fla. Rule of Civ. Proc. 1.510*, 317 So.3d 72, 75–77 (Fla. 2021). Furthermore, establishing the threshold existence and unambiguous applicability of an enforceable contract governing the specific project location is a mandatory prerequisite to enforcing any contractual duty to defend or indemnify. *Nichols v. Vill. Park Mobile Home Ests., Inc.*, 237 So.2d 807, 808 (Fla. 2d DCA 1970). Pursuant to Fla. Stat. § 725.06(1), any construction contract indemnification or defense provision is strictly "void and unenforceable" unless the contract contains a monetary limitation on indemnification that "bears a reasonable commercial relationship to the contract." § 725.06(1), Fla. Stat. (2025). Florida jurisprudence dictates that a party seeking summary enforcement of contractual indemnity and defense obligations must establish that the underlying contract unambiguously encompasses the subject project and location (Nichols, 237 So.2d at 808). Where contracts contain geographic, divisional, or structural ambiguities or fail to satisfy the strict commercial reasonableness requirements of Fla. Stat. § 725.06(1) summary disposition is improper as a matter of law.

The Record on Appeal conclusively demonstrates an irreconcilable divisional mismatch and foundational incompleteness that precludes summary judgment. Specifically, D.R. Horton relies on a master "Florida Independent Contractor Agreement" executed on August 1, 2011, which on its face is explicitly restricted to D.R. Horton's "Tampa" division (R. 62). In stark contrast, the subject single-family residence located in Orlando, Florida, was constructed in Orange County under D.R. Horton's separate Orlando divisions, as confirmed by the affidavits of Orlando East Division Land Development Manager Louis Avelli and Central Florida Division Operations Manager Ryan Kramer (R. 59, 104,). Although D.R. Horton points to Purchase Order No. 207006 issued for the home, the underlying master agreement is incomplete on its face and fails to establish that Tampa administrative terms legally bound Orlando municipal operations (R. 62–75). This fatal uncertainty regarding the operative contract is further established by the sworn admissions of D.R. Horton's own designated corporate representative, Joseph N. Tucker, who testified under oath that he could not identify the proffered contract as relating to the work performed on the Ramjit project:

> Q. We talked about it earlier, you, sitting here today, cannot identify the contract that would have guided -- that would have related to the work between D.R. Horton and ProBuild at the Ramjit project, correct?
>
> A. Not -- yeah, not the one that was shown to me.
>
> Q. The one shown to you, you cannot identify as being related to the work, correct?
>
> A. I cannot.
>
> Deposition of Joseph N. Tucker as Corporate Representative for D.R. Horton, Inc., pg. 32 ln 3-10 (R.169)

This lack of contractual alignment and corporate uncertainty surrounding the identity, scope, and enforceability of the operative contract present genuinely contested issues of fact and demonstrate a disconnect with the project site. Consequently, D.R. Horton cannot establish compliance with the commercial reasonableness and monetary limitation mandates of Fla. Stat. § 725.06(1). Because the record contains conflicting contractual documents, an unresolvable divisional mismatch regarding the project site, and undisputed testimony showing that D.R. Horton's own corporate representative could not identify any governing contract for the Project (R. 170), D.R. Horton failed to establish the threshold existence of an applicable contract as a matter of law. Accordingly, granting summary judgment was reversible error (R. 62,).

## D.R. Horton's Designated Corporate Representative's Binding Admissions Under Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.310(B)(6) Preclude Summary Judgment.

The lower tribunal erred in entering summary judgment because D.R. Horton's designated corporate representative made binding sworn admissions under Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.310(b)(6) confirming an inability to confirm or produce a contract for the Orlando division, and explicitly admitting an inability to identify any governing contract or confirm whether the Tampa-division agreement applied to the Ramjit project, thereby generating genuine issues of material fact that cannot be overcome by contradictory pre-deposition affidavits (R. 1161–1262).

Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.310(b)(6)—the substance of which was borrowed from Fed. R. Civ. P. 30(b)(6)—mandates that a corporate party designated for deposition reasonably prepare its representative to testify regarding specified subject matters and provide "complete, knowledgeable, and binding answers on behalf of the corporation." *Plantation-Simon Inc. v. Bahloul*, 596 So.2d 1159, 1160 (Fla. 4th DCA 1992); *Carriage Hills Condo., Inc. v. JBH Roofing & Constructors, Inc.*, 109 So.3d 329, 334 (Fla. 4th DCA 2013); *Churchill v. DBI Servs., LLC*, 361 So.3d 896, 899 (Fla. 3d DCA 2023). The leading Florida case on this doctrine is *Carriage Hills Condo., Inc. v. JBH Roofing & Constructors, Inc.*, 109 So.3d 329, 334 (Fla. 4th DCA 2013). The Fourth District Court of Appeal held that under Rule 1.310(b)(6), "the person(s) designated to testify represents the collective knowledge of the corporation, not of the individual deponents." *Id.* at 334. Adopting the precise language from federal precedent, the court explained that as the corporation's "voice," the witness does "not simply testify about matters within his or her personal knowledge but rather is 'speaking for the corporation.'" *Id.* at 334–35. The court further held that "the corporation appears vicariously through its designees." *Id.* at 335. While such testimony does not constitute an immutable judicial admission that permanently estops the corporation from offering contradictory evidence at trial *Id.* When properly noticed, the designee's testimony is deemed to be the testimony of the corporation itself and commits the entity to a position at that point in time. *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. New Horizont, Inc.*, 250 F.R.D. 203, 212 (E.D. Pa. 2008). While such testimony does not constitute an immutable judicial admission that permanently estops the corporation from offering contradictory evidence at trial *Id.*, a corporate party "when met by a motion for summary judgment, may not repudiate or contradict by affidavit [its] previous deposition testimony so as to create a jury issue" without a valid, credible explanation. *Cary v. Keene Corp.*, 472 So. 2d 851, 853 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985); *Ellison v. Anderson*, 74 So.2d 680, 681 (Fla. 1954); *Croft v. York*, 244 So.2d 161, 165 (Fla. 1st DCA 1971). Florida courts strictly enforce the rule that a litigant cannot manufacture a sham issue of fact to defeat summary judgment by submitting a late affidavit that flatly contradicts earlier binding deposition testimony given by its designated corporate representative (*Cary*, 472 So.2d at 853). However, where an affidavit is the earlier sworn statement and a deposition directly undermines it, courts apply the same logic in reverse. In *Slominski v. Citizens Prop. Ins.*, 99 So. 3d 973 (Fla. 4th DCA 2012), the court refused to allow the party to rely on affidavits that were "inconsistent with their prior deposition testimony," holding that where deposition testimony directly contradicts affidavit statements, the party "was not entitled to rely upon them in opposition to summary judgment" *Id.* at 976. Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.310(b)(6) testimony establishes the corporate baseline, binding the entity unless properly clarified or corrected through recognized evidentiary mechanisms.

Tucker, admitted under oath as the official "voice" of the entity that the corporation could neither produce nor confirm a contract specifically executed for the Orlando division, and further conceded an inability to state with certainty that the Tampa-limited agreement governed the Home (R.169). Where the movant's designated corporate witness admits uncertainty regarding the foundational contract as the official voice of the entity and confirms an inability to identify any governing contract for the project (R. 169-170), genuine issues of material fact exist, rendering summary judgment reversible error.

## The Voluntary Dismissal of Count IV and the Absence of Any Allegation of Workmanship Defects Attributable to ProBuild Relieve ProBuild of Any Duty to Defend as a Matter of Law.

The trial court erred as a matter of law in enforcing a contractual duty to defend where the sole underlying count implicating window selections was voluntarily dismissed prior to answer, the remaining claims bore no factual nexus to ProBuild's scope of work, and binding corporate admissions confirm the total absence of any installation execution claims.

A contractual duty to defend is evaluated strictly based on the allegations contained within the four corners of the underlying complaint. *Metro. Dade County v. CBM Indus. of Minn., Inc.*, 776 So.2d 937, 938 (Fla. 3d DCA 2000). Where the underlying claims do not implicate the subcontractor's actual scope of work, materials, or workmanship, the duty to defend is not triggered. *Id.* The "four corners" rule limits the duty to defend to the active, live allegations asserted in the underlying litigation *Id.* If the claims touching upon a subcontractor's scope are voluntarily dismissed or abandoned, and remaining claims fail to allege any defect in the subcontractor's actual workmanship or field execution, the duty to defend is extinguished as a matter of law.

The Record on Appeal demonstrates that the underlying claims against D.R. Horton were strictly rooted in D.R. Horton's independent product choice—specifically, the selection of "C" Channel windows and architectural design specifications which fell within D.R. Horton's exclusive discretion. The expert report prepared by Barry J. Smith, P.E., of Pinnacle Engineering focused entirely on stucco thickness, casing beads, and architectural detailing (R.76-96) rather than defective window installation or workmanship by ProBuild (R.803–810). Crucially, on October 13, 2021, Ramjit voluntarily dismissed Count IV of her Complaint (the sole count addressing window type selection) before D.R. Horton ever filed its answer (R.180). Subsequently, D.R. Horton dropped its direct claims for breach of contract and common law indemnity, leaving only an isolated claim for an alleged duty to indemnify and defend (R. 411–412, 515–524).

This complete absence of any claim regarding installation execution or workmanship is conclusively established by the sworn admissions of D.R. Horton's designated corporate representative, Joseph N. Tucker. When confronted with the allegations of the underlying complaint, Mr. Tucker was questioned directly regarding the scope of Ms. Ramjit's allegations:

# CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the final judgment and partial summary judgment entered in favor of D.R. Horton, Inc., vacate any award of attorneys' fees and costs against ProBuild, and remand with instructions to enter final judgment in favor of ProBuild (R. 420).

Summary judgment was improper for three independent and dispositive reasons. First, genuine issues of material fact preclude a finding that an enforceable contract governed the Orlando construction project. D.R. Horton's reliance on a Florida Independent Contractor Agreement, explicitly restricted to its Tampa division, creates an unresolvable divisional mismatch with the Orange County project managed by its Orlando divisions, rendering the agreement legally insufficient and non-compliant with the statutory commercial reasonableness and monetary limitation mandates of Florida Statute § 725.06(1) (R. 59, 62–74, 104,). Second, this foundational contractual gap was cemented by D.R. Horton's own designated corporate representative, who testified under oath pursuant to Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.310(b)(6), that the corporation could neither locate nor confirm an Orlando-specific contract binding admissions that D.R. Horton cannot unilaterally erase or contradict through self-serving pre-deposition affidavits to manufacture a feigned issue of fact (R. 59–143, 1161–1262). Third, even if a binding agreement were presumed, ProBuild's contractual duty to defend was never triggered. The underlying litigation lacked any surviving allegations connecting ProBuild's actual workmanship or materials to actionable defects particularly after Ramjit voluntarily dismissed Count IV before answering leaving zero factual nexus to support D.R. Horton's indemnity and defense claims (R.411–412, 515–524, 803–810).

Accordingly, the judgment below must be reversed and rendered in favor of Appellant.
