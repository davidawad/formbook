# STATEMENT OF ISSUES

The issue presented for review is whether the Housing Court, in a summary process action, correctly found that the landlord’s tender of full compensation to the tenant for the tenant’s claim under G.L. c. 186, § 22, the water use statute, and the tenant’s acceptance of such compensation without reservation of rights, satisfied and resolved the claim such that it can not be used to trigger a defense to possession under G.L. c. 239, § 8A.

# ARGUMENT

## I. Introduction

The Housing Court correctly concluded that the landlord’s tender of funds to the tenant prior to trial, which the tenant accepted without reservation, satisfied any potential damages under G.L. c. 186, § 22, such that the tenant could not then assert such a claim at trial to trigger the application of G.L. c. 239, § 8A as a defense to possession. RA 27. The Section 8A defense to possession was not available as there was no “amount” that the court could find “to be

due the tenant” by reason of the counterclaim for alleged violation of G.L. c. 186, § 22.

## II. Standard of Review

The standard of review applicable to issues of statutory construction, as questions of law, is de novo. Commerce Ins. Co. v. Commissioner of Ins., 447 Mass. 478, 481 (2006). A reviewing court’s “primary duty in interpreting a statute is to effectuate the intent of the Legislature in enacting it.” Allegaert v. Harbor View Hotel Owner LLC, 180 N.E.3d 459, 463 (Mass. App. Ct. 2021), citing and quoting Spencer v. Civil Serv. Comm’n, 479 Mass. 210, 216 (2018) (quotation marks omitted). “Ordinarily, where the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is conclusive as to legislative intent.” *Id.*, citing Abuzahra v. Cambridge, 486 Mass. 818, 822 (2021) (quotation omitted). “[W]here the statutory language is ambiguous or unclear, we consider the cause of its enactment, the mischief or imperfection to be remedied and the main object to be accomplished, [such that] the purpose of its framers may be effectuated.” *Id.* (citation and quotation omitted).

However, the more narrow “clear error” standard of review applies to findings of fact made by a judge,

particularly where the findings rest on credibility determinations. An appellate court is “bound by a judge’s findings of fact that are supported by the evidence, including all inferences that may reasonably be drawn from the evidence...A finding of fact by the trial judge will not be deemed ‘clearly erroneous’ unless the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” White v. Hartigan, 464 Mass. 400, 414 (2013) (citations omitted).

Finally, the narrow “abuse of discretion” standard of review applies to rulings that require application of legal principles to a set of facts. Great deference is afforded to a judge’s exercise of discretion. L.L. v. Commonwealth, 470 Mass. 169, 185 at fn. 27 (2014). An abuse of discretion occurs where a judge makes a clear error of judgement in weighing the factors relevant to a discretionary decision such that the decision falls outside the range of reasonable alternatives. *Id.*




## III. The Defense to Possession Under Section 8A Was Unavailable as There Was No Amount That Could Be Found by the Court “To Be Due” to the Tenant for the Alleged Violation of Law

Contrary to Ms. Charland’s argument, a tenant cannot assert the defense to possession under G.L. c. 239, § 8A merely by demonstrating a claim against or violation of law by the landlord. The plain language of the statute requires a finding by the trial court that there is some amount, greater than zero, presently due and owing to the tenant by reason of a counterclaim permitted under the statute. The fifth paragraph of G.L. c. 239, § 8A, provides as follows, with the first two sentences separated here:

There shall be no recovery of possession under this chapter if the amount found by the court to be due the landlord equals or is less than the amount found to be due the tenant or occupant by reason of any counterclaim or defense under this section. If the amount found to be due the landlord exceeds the amount found to be due the tenant or occupant, there shall be no recovery of possession if the tenant or occupant, within one week after having received written notice from the court of the balance due, pays to the clerk the balance due the landlord, together with interest and costs of suit, less any credit due the tenant or occupant for funds already paid by him to the clerk under this section. G.L. c. 239, § 8A, fifth para. (emphasis added).

Pursuant to the plain language of the statute, first sentence, a valid defense to a landlord’s claim

of possession requires a finding by the trial court of some amount of money due and owing to the tenant, either in an amount equal to what is owed to the landlord or greater than what is owed to the landlord. The use of the words “to be due” the landlord and “to be due” the tenant signifies amounts that are, as of yet, unpaid to the other party. The Legislature utilized specific language referencing the present status, not the past, with a presumption of future payment. So, for example, if the court finds that the amount due to the landlord is $100 and the amount due to the tenant is $100, then the tenant keeps possession, under the first sentence. Further, if the court finds that the amount due to the landlord is $50 and the amount due to the tenant is $100, then the tenant keeps possession, under the first sentence. Moreover, if the court finds that the amount due to the landlord is $100 and the amount due to the tenant is $50, then the tenant has the opportunity to pay the difference (plus interest and costs) within one week and retain possession, under the second sentence.

However, if there is no amount to be found due the landlord and there is also no amount to be found due the tenant, then the tenant may not keep

possession under Section 8A. Zeros on both sides of the analysis cannot trigger the tenant’s defense to possession. If this were not the case, then an anomalous result would be permitted where, for example, in a no-fault case where there is no unpaid rent or use and occupancy, and where the tenant has asserted a Section 8A counterclaim but loses on that counterclaim at trial (perhaps the court finds that a minor condition at the premises does not amount to a breach of the warranty of habitability), then the tenant would still get to keep possession despite losing her counterclaim. Such an outcome could not have been intended.

In other words, if there is no amount due and owing to the landlord and no amount due and owing to the tenant, the tenant cannot assert the defense to possession. A tenant cannot defeat a landlord’s claim to possession under Section 8A merely by the tenant raising a claim for a violation of law intended for the protection of tenant’s rights in the landlord-tenant relationship. A tenant has to prove and be owed some amount of damages.

This interpretation of the statute aligns with the broader concept of damages. In general, an element

of a claim is damages and damages must be proved. Here, Ms. Charland did not prove any damages at trial. She did not present any evidence of damages related to the water use issue. RA 60-82. In fact, her counsel conceded at trial that Ms. Charland has been made whole by Ms. Ferreira’s payments. RA 39. See *Kattar v. Demoulas*, 433 Mass. 1, 15-16 (2000) (“A fundamental principle on which the rule of damages is based is compensation. Compensation is that amount of money that reasonably will make the injured party whole. Compensatory damages may not exceed this amount. Anything beyond that amount is a windfall.”) A plaintiff must sustain her burden of proving the amount of her loss with reasonable certainty. Agoos Leather Cos. v. Am. & Foreign Ins. Co., 342 Mass. 603, 608 (1961). Damages must be reasonably ascertainable from the evidence. Dalton v. Demos Bros. Gen. Contractors, Inc., 334 Mass. 377, 378 (1956). See also Hershenow v. Enterprise Rent-a-Car Company of Boston, Inc., 445 Mass. 790 (2006).

To the extent that Ms. Charland attempts to argue in her brief on appeal that Ms. Ferreira failed to compensate her for attorney’s fees, the argument is unavailing. First and foremost, Ms. Charland did not

raise the issue of attorney’s fees below and, as a consequence, she cannot raise it for the first time on appeal. SRA 10-15; RA 36-37, 39-41, 91-92. “An issue not raised or argued below may not be argued for the first time on appeal.” Carey v. New England Organ Bank, 446 Mass. 270, 285 (2006), quoting Century Fire & Marine Ins. Corp. v. Bank of New England-Bristol County, N.A., 405 Mass. 420, 421 at fn. 2 (1989) (quotation marks omitted). “In order to raise an issue on appeal, a party must lodge a specific objection on the record.” Ciccarelli v. School Dept. of Lowell, 70 Mass. App. Ct. 787, 799 (2007), citing Mass. R. Civ. P. 46. Second, Ms. Charland is not entitled to attorney’s fees on her counterclaim based on the water use statute as the statute does not specifically provide for same. G.L. c. 186, § 22. Nor was Ms. Charland eligible for attorney’s fees under G.L. c. 186, § 14 or G.L. c. 93A, as she brought neither a breach of quiet enjoyment claim nor a consumer protection claim based on the water use issue. RA 12. Finally, Ms. Charland cannot assert a claim to attorney’s fees in order to establish damages for purposes of the calculation of amounts due under Section 8A. Except in very narrow circumstances,

attorney’s fees do not constitute damages. See *Columbia Chiropractic Group, Inc. v. Trust Ins. Co.*, 430 Mass. 60, 63 (1999), citing *Jet Line Servs., Inc. v. American Employers Ins. Co.*, 404 Mass. 706, 718 (1989). As such, the landlord was not required to pay the tenant any attorney’s fees. Krock v. Krock, 46 Mass. App. Ct. 528, 531 at fn. 2 (1999) (“As a general rule in Massachusetts, a litigant must bear his own expenses including attorney’s fees, except where a statute permits the award of costs, a valid contract [or] stipulation provides for costs, or rules concerning damages permit [] recovery”)(citation omitted).

Even if the language of Section 8A might be construed as ambiguous when applied to the scenario at issue here, the intent of the Legislature in enacting the statute, and its legislative history, indicate a desire to protect tenants, but not a desire to punish landlords. In fact, the section protects the landlord’s right to the fair value of use and occupation of the premises and provides for payment procedures pending the summary process action, as well as providing that, where a tenant owes the landlord a balance due and elects to take advantage of the

statutory 7-day payment option, the tenant still must pay the landlord’s costs of suit and interest. And while the legislative history reflects “an intent to provide tenants with a broad set of defenses and counterclaims in the summary process action,” *Meikle v. Nurse*, 474 Mass. 207, 213-214 (2016), this does not mean that the Legislature intended that a tenant may defeat possession when a landlord corrects a potential economic error prior to trial and compensates the tenant more than the charges paid for water and sewer. The violation here, a lack of water conservation devices, did not impact the availability, quality or condition of water provided to the premises during the tenancy. The only impact was a potential consumption of an amount of water at a rate higher than if low-flow devices had been in place. Again, those entire charges were more than reimbursed to the tenants, leaving nothing left to litigate. There were no damages to account for.

Ms. Ferreira is not a professional landlord and would not fall into the category of those landlords and property owners in the business of renting residential premises for commercial profit and who are the focus of much of the legislation around regulating

residential tenancies. Summary process is intended to be expeditious, and its procedure should not be manipulated into protracted proceedings which have the effect of withholding rightful possession of a residential property from its owner. See *Hodge v. Klug*, 33 Mass. App. Ct. 746 (1992) and Brown, J. (concurring opinion). The Defendant “must surely [] appreciate[] that the owner of a residential property such as this one was entitled to end an informal arrangement for the occupation of [her home], and that the case would end in a judgment so providing.” *Hodge v. Klug*, 33 Mass. App. Ct. at 758.

Moreover, to read Section 8A in the manner argued by Ms. Charland in these circumstances would discourage landlords from promptly correcting errors, in particular technical errors where the potential remedy is fairly calculable, after a tenant brings the issue to her attention. A landlord would be disincentivized to promptly correct the oversight after notice of a complaint if she still could not regain possession after compensating the tenant for the oversight. This interpretation would have the effect of discouraging proactive behavior and resolution where we generally favor encouraging such

settlement and resolution of legal issues. This policy is reflected in such rules and laws as Mass. R. Civ. P. 68 concerning offers of judgment which encourage offers where liability is clear in order to shift responsibility for further litigation costs; and G.L. c. 93A concerning an offer of settlement in response to a 30-day demand letter which can potentially limit the amount recoverable by a plaintiff if the court finds the offer reasonable. Mass. R. Civ. P. 68; G.L. c. 93A, § 9(3).

Ms. Charland recognizes that the Housing Court has held that upon a landlord’s payment of statutory damages due under G.L. c. 186, § 15B, upon demand by the tenant, the tenant cannot successfully assert a defense to possession under Section 8A. *Cruz v. Norat-Reyes*, 17-SP-181, March 1, 2017 (Housing Ct., Western Div.)(Fields, J.). It is accurate that the security deposit statute specifically sets forth a “cure opportunity” to a landlord whereby the tenant’s claim for damages arises only if the landlord fails to return to the tenant the forfeited security deposit upon demand. The water use statute has no comparable cure language. However, it must also be noted that the water use statute also does not contain any specific

penalty provisions for a landlord’s failure to adhere to certain requirements for charging tenants for their water use, while the security deposit statute provides hefty penalty provisions for treble damages, costs and attorney’s fees. In other words, the Legislature specifically laid out detailed penalty provisions in the security deposit statute because it “views violations of the security deposit statute, G.L. c. 186, § 15B, as serious violations.” *Meikle v. Nurse*, 474 Mass. 207, 213 (2016). It cannot be that a landlord’s remedying of a serious security deposit claim upon demand will render obsolete a tenant’s defense to possession under Section 8A, while a landlord’s remedying of a minor, technical violation of the water use statute will nonetheless allow a tenant to retain possession even where there is nothing owed to the tenant.

To the extent the court exercised any discretion, it did not abuse that discretion in applying Section 8A. Given the equities at issue and the relatively minor and technical violation of the water use statute that did not concern an issue of habitability or quiet enjoyment, being merely economic in nature, and where the landlord reimbursed arguably much more than the

tenant could have received as damages, the court was within its discretion to find the defense inapplicable and/or waived and to grant possession to the landlord. The Supreme Judicial Court, in analyzing a security deposit counterclaim, has interpreted “the Legislature’s intent in providing for the tenant’s right to retain possession as a time-limited equitable remedy for the particular conduct underlying the tenant’s defense or counterclaim.” *Meikle v. Nurse*, 474 Mass. 207, 214 (2016). Here, there was no abuse of discretion where the only counterclaims related to the security deposit and the water use, the tenants were fully compensated for both, and the landlord desperately needed her home back, when the court finally issued a judgment for possession.

For all of the foregoing reasons, Ms. Charland’s claim under G.L. c. 186, § 22 was moot and could not be used to trigger a defense to possession under G.L. c. 239, § 8A.

## IV. The Tenant Accepted the Reimbursement Without Reservation of Rights and the Water Use Claim Was Thereby Satisfied and Resolved

Ms. Charland attempts to argue that typical contract principles should apply in this case and that

she did not expressly agree to resolve her claim. As argued above, pursuant to the plain language of Section 8A, Ms. Charland was simply not able to assert the defense to possession as she was not owed any amounts. Therefore, an analysis of offer/acceptance is not applicable. However, assuming arguendo that some typical contract principles might apply in this situation, Ms. Charland indeed received the funds, she did not return the funds to the landlord, she did not accept the funds with a reservation of any rights, and she has received legal representation. Indeed, she conceded at trial, through counsel, that she had been made whole. She took no steps to preserve a potential defense to possession at trial. In these circumstances, the court did not err when it ruled that the landlord “tendered all the funds for the damages asserted under [the water use] claim and that they were knowingly accepted by the tenants without any reservation of rights” and that “such tender and acceptance satisfied and resolved the tenants’ claim under G.L. c. 186, s. 22[.]” RA 27. These findings were supported by the evidence and argument at trial. Ms. Charland offered no evidence of any rejection of the funds or any reservation of rights.

Ms. Charland cites *Henry v. Bozzuto Mgmt. Co.*, 98 Mass. App. Ct. 690 (2020), for the proposition that even where the amount of damages has been resolved and accounted for, judgment on the legal claim still should enter. The case is distinguishable, however, for the simple fact that the case does not address the unique and important issue of possession of residential premises in a summary process case; it concerned claims for violation of the security deposit statute and Chapter 93A. The case is further distinguished by the fact that the former tenants there specifically rejected both the property manager’s settlement offer made in response to the Chapter 93A demand letter and thereafter rejected the property manager’s tender of the amount demanded by the tenants to settle their individual claims (the property manager also never made an offer to resolve the purported demand on behalf of all similarly situated former tenants). Here, Ms. Charland did not reject the payments made by Ms. Ferreira, either affirmatively or impliedly. There was no evidence in the record that she returned the payments or informed Ms. Ferreira’s counsel that she rejected the tender. Ms. Charland retained the payments. Now, Ms. Charland

wants to have her cake and eat it too by retaining the benefits of the compensation and by claiming a defense to possession nonetheless. However, she is estopped from doing so.

Finally, Ms. Charland cites to *Meikle v. Nurse* in her brief to console landlords with the decision’s clarification that Section 8A “does not impose an obligatory tenancy on the landlord. Nothing in the statute prevents the landlord from bringing a second summary process action for possession after he or she has remedied the violation of the security deposit statute.” *Meikle v. Nurse*, 474 Mass. 207, 214 (2016). In the circumstances of this matter, the proposition is cold comfort. Ms. Ferreira indeed did commence a second no-fault summary process action to regain possession of her home, despite having won possession in the first case. However, the Housing Court has stayed those proceedings at Ms. Charland’s request pending the outcome of her appeal in this action, further delaying the moment when Ms. Ferreira will be

able to move back into her home. See Western Division Housing Court Docket No. 21-SP-3526. 3

# CONCLUSION

For all the foregoing reasons, the Plaintiff-Appellee, Cassandra Ferreira, hereby requests that the judgment of the Housing Court granting possession of the premises to the landlord be affirmed.
