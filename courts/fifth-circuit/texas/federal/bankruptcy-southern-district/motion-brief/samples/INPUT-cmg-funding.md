# I. PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

The Debtor filed this Chapter 11 case on August 24, 2026, and immediately made plans to spend its secured lender’s cash without consent, without a court order, and without offering any form of adequate protection.

CMG Funding 2025 LLC holds a valid, perfected first-priority security interest in substantially all of the Debtor’s property, including all accounts receivable, and all proceeds thereof, securing the Prepetition Obligations. CMG’s security interest was duly perfected before the Petition Date through UCC-1 financing statements filed with the Texas Secretary of State and control agreements over deposit accounts maintained at JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.

The Bankruptcy Code is unambiguous: a debtor may not use cash collateral unless the secured creditor consents or the Court authorizes such use after notice, a hearing, and a finding of adequate protection. The Debtor has satisfied none of these requirements. Rather, on August 25, 2026, it filed its Emergency Motion for Entry of Interim and Final Orders Authorizing Use of Cash Collateral, to the Extent Applicable, and Granting Related Relief (the “Cash Collateral Motion”) [Ch. 11 ECF No. 13], purporting to dispute CMG’s security interests and liens on the Debtor’s Collateral. CMG has not consented to any use of its Cash Collateral, and the Debtor has not obtained any order from this Court authorizing the use of Cash Collateral on terms that protect CMG’s interests. Every dollar of Cash Collateral the Debtor asserts is unencumbered and may be spent without adequate protection permanently erodes the collateral base securing the Prepetition Obligations, inflicting irreparable harm that no after-the-fact damages award can remedy. The harm is not speculative, and the Debtor’s compliance with section 363 of the Bankruptcy Code and recognition of CMG’s valid, perfected and first-priority security interests is warranted.

CMG satisfies all four prerequisites for a preliminary injunction under the Fifth Circuit’s well-settled standard: a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm absent relief, a balance of equities tipping decisively in CMG’s favor, and furtherance of the public interest in enforcing the Bankruptcy Code’s cash collateral protections. The relief CMG seeks is narrow and preserves—rather than disrupts—the status quo: it simply requires the Debtor to comply with section 363(c)(2) of the Bankruptcy Code before spending CMG’s cash. Although the Debtor has now filed the Cash Collateral Motion, the relief sought therein falls woefully short of adequately protecting CMG’s interest, and, in any event, is by its title only sought “to the extent applicable,” based on the Debtor’s disingenuous refusal to acknowledge the secured interests provided to CMG under the documents governing the Prepetition Obligations.

CMG respectfully urges this Court to act swiftly to restrain the Debtor’s proposed dissipation of Cash Collateral and to enter the proposed order enjoining such use absent CMG’s written consent or a Court order providing adequate protection.

# V. ARGUMENT

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 65, made applicable to adversary proceedings in bankruptcy by Federal Rule of Bankruptcy Procedure 7065, allows a bankruptcy court to issue temporary restraining orders and preliminary injunctions. See FED. R. BANKR. P. 7065 (“Fed. R. Civ. P. 65 applies in an adversary proceeding.”); FED. R. CIV. P. 65 (empowering courts to issue temporary restraining orders and preliminary injunctions).

Section 105 of the Bankruptcy Code, moreover, authorizes the bankruptcy court to “issue any order, process, or judgment that is necessary or appropriate to carry out the provisions of [the Bankruptcy Code].” 11 U.S.C. § 105. Under this authority, bankruptcy courts may “fashion . . . orders as are necessary to further the substantive provisions of the Bankruptcy Code.” Coie v. Sadkin (In re Sadkin), 36 F.3d 473, 478 (5th Cir. 1994) (quoting Chiasson v. Bingler (In re Oxford Mgmt. Inc.), 4 F.3d 1329, 1333 (5th Cir. 1993)).

To be entitled to a preliminary injunction, a movant must demonstrate the following: (i) a substantial likelihood that the movant will prevail on the merits; (ii) a substantial threat that the movant will suffer irreparable injury if the injunction is not granted; (iii) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the threatened harm an injunction may cause the party opposing the injunction; and (iv) that the granting of the injunction will not disserve the public interest. Texas v. United States Dep’t of Homeland Sec., 123 F.4th 186, 198 (5th Cir. 2024). Each factor favors an injunction here.

### A. There Is A Substantial Likelihood CMG Will Prevail On The Merits.

The first factor—likelihood of success—is satisfied because CMG is likely to succeed on the merits of its claim that it holds a valid, perfected security interest in the Cash Collateral and that the Debtor cannot use such Cash Collateral absent CMG’s consent or adequate protection. To show a likelihood of success on the merits, a movant “must present a prima facie case, but need not prove that they are entitled to summary judgment.” TitleMax of Texas, Inc. v. City of Dallas, 142 F.4th 322, 329 (5th Cir. 2025) (quoting Daniels Health Scis., L.L.C. v. Vascular Health Scis., L.L.C., 710 F.3d 579, 582 (5th Cir. 2013)) (cleaned up).

As set forth in further detail in the Adversary Complaint, CMG holds an interest in the Debtor’s Cash by virtue of its prepetition security interest and liens on the Debtor’s deposit accounts, accounts receivable, inventory, and other Collateral, and on the proceeds thereof, granted under the Loan Documents. The Loan Documents, together with the properly filed UCC-1 financing statements and any control agreements over deposit accounts, evidence and perfect CMG’s security interest and liens in the Cash and the underlying Collateral. Under section 363(a) of the Bankruptcy Code, “cash collateral” includes “cash, negotiable instruments, documents of title, securities, deposit accounts, or other cash equivalents whenever acquired in which the estate and an entity other than the estate have an interest.” 11 U.S.C. § 363(a). Accordingly, the Debtor’s Cash is “cash collateral” under section 363(a) of the Bankruptcy Code, that CMG—as an “entity other than the estate”—has an interest in. 11 U.S.C. § 363(a). Under the express terms of section 363(c)(2) of the Bankruptcy Code, the Debtor therefore may not use the Cash Collateral unless CMG either consents to such use, or the Court, after notice and a hearing, authorizes such use. 11 U.S.C. § 363(c)(2).

CMG’s liens are valid, binding, enforceable, and perfected against the Debtor and its estate. CMG has more than carried its burden to show a likelihood of success on the merits of its claim for Bankruptcy Code protections of the Cash Collateral.

### B. If The Motion Is Not Granted, CMG Will Suffer Irreparable Harm.

The second factor—irreparable harm—is met because CMG will suffer immediate and irreparable harm if the Debtor, as argued in the Cash Collateral Motion, is able to ignore the validity of the Loan Documents and CMG’s perfected security interests, and use Cash Collateral without adequate protection or CMG’s consent. The Debtor’s proposed unauthorized use reduces the Collateral pool securing CMG’s claim and impairs CMG’s ability to be repaid by the Debtor.

In the Cash Collateral Motion, the Debtor “disputes the amount, validity, extent, enforceability, priority, and perfection of certain asserted liens and claims, including any asserted lien on IOLTA funds, unearned fees, future recoveries, case, client agreements, co-counsel agreements, and other disputed categories of property.” See Cash Collateral Motion at ¶ 22. The Debtor’s statements in the Cash Collateral Motion repudiate what the Debtor expressly agreed to in the Loan Documents, and demonstrate the Debtor’s intent to ignore CMG’s security interests and use the Cash in part to fund the Chapter 11 Case.

Injunctive relief is an extraordinary remedy, but Cash Collateral is not merely an ordinary estate asset; it is the most liquid asset that a debtor’s estate may hold, and therefore lends a secured creditor the greatest chance of being returned the dollar value of its secured claim. The Bankruptcy Code therefore places special restrictions on its use, which should not and cannot be circumvented by a debtor’s refusal to recognize it as such. Indeed, when “secured creditors (under the UCC or otherwise) seek court intervention to maintain their position, the prospective loss of their status quo security interest has been held sufficient to constitute the irreparable harm needed to justify an injunction.” Plainfield Specialty Holdings II Inc. v. Children’s Legal Servs. PLLC, 634 F. Supp. 2d 833, 846 (E.D. Mich. 2009); see also Temsa Ulasim Araclari Sanayi Ve Ticaret A.S. v. CH Bus Sales, LLC, No. CV 18-698-RGA, 2018 WL 4179456, at *3 (D. Del. Aug. 31, 2018) (finding creditor “has demonstrated a sufficient threat to its security interest” warranting preliminary injunction to prevent loss or destruction of creditor’s collateral).

A harm is irreparable “where there is no adequate remedy at law, such as monetary damages.” Janvey v. Alguire, 647 F.3d 585, 600 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing Deerfield Med. Ctr. v. City of Deerfield Beach, 661 F.2d 328, 338 (5th Cir. 1981)). In this case, only an order restraining the Debtor’s use of the Cash Collateral will prevent harm to CMG. On the face of the Cash Collateral Motion, it would seem that the Debtor is offering CMG adequate protection, but the Debtor is not. Replacement liens only to the extent set forth in the proposed order attached to the Cash Collateral Motion are not a meaningful remedy, especially given CMG’s existing security interests in the Debtor’s assets. See Cash Collateral Motion at ¶ 13.

On information and belief, no other creditor holds a lien senior to CMG’s lien in such Cash, meaning that CMG specifically will suffer harm. Every dollar of Cash Collateral spent without adequate protection permanently reduces the Collateral base securing CMG’s claim, causing irreparable harm. The Debtor’s proposed use of CMG’s Cash Collateral without either consent or adequate protection deprives CMG of the very protection the Bankruptcy Code requires before that collateral may be utilized to administer the estate.

### C. The Threatened Injury To CMG Outweighs The Threatened Harm To The Debtor.

The balance of harms favors entry of a preliminary injunction. The injunctive relief requested in this Motion is narrow; it would not determine the full amount of CMG’s claim, grant final relief on all lien issues, or bar the Debtor from seeking authority to use the Cash Collateral or obtain financing. Simply, the relief would preserve the status quo by prohibiting the Debtor from using CMG’s cash collateral unless CMG consents, or the Debtor obtains authority from this Court after providing adequate protection to CMG. The Debtor remains free to seek Court authorization to use Cash Collateral on terms that adequately protect CMG, or to obtain alternative financing that preserves CMG’s bargained-for security interest in its Collateral. CMG merely seeks to preserve its Collateral position pending such determination or resolution.

Alternatively, as stated above, the Debtor’s proposed use of Cash Collateral to pay the expenses associated with the Chapter 11 Case, while ignoring the legally binding Loan Documents it executed in favor of CMG in exchange for approximately $45 million, will immediately and irreparably diminish CMG’s Collateral and jeopardize CMG’s ability to be repaid on its Prepetition Obligations. CMG cannot afford to wait for a decision on the merits of the Adversary Complaint before the Debtor is prohibited from using the Cash Collateral without appropriate safeguards, which warrants immediate injunctive relief.

### D. Granting The Preliminary Injunction Will Benefit Public Interest.

Finally, the fourth factor—public interest—also supports granting the requested injunction. The requested relief is consistent with the Bankruptcy Code’s structure regarding cash collateral, protects CMG’s bargained-for security interests, and promotes orderly administration of the estate in compliance with the express provisions of the Bankruptcy Code.

CMG filed the Adversary Complaint, seeking a declaration of the secured status of CMG’s claim against the Debtor. Pending the resolution of the Adversary Complaint, it would benefit the public interest to maintain the status quo and prevent the proposed unauthorized use of the Cash Collateral as sought by the Debtor in the Cash Collateral Motion.

Cash collateral includes cash, negotiable instruments, deposit accounts, other cash equivalents, and the proceeds, products, offspring, rents, or profits of property in which both the estate and another entity have an interest. A debtor may not use cash collateral unless the creditor with an interest in the collateral consents or the court, after notice and a hearing, authorizes such use. 11 U.S.C. § 363(c)(2). If the creditor does not consent, cash collateral may be used only to the extent the court determines that the creditor’s interest is adequately protected. The Debtor bears the burden of proving that a secured creditor is adequately protected for purposes of using cash collateral.

The Debtor does not have CMG’s consent to use Cash Collateral. CMG expressly withholds consent to any use of its Cash Collateral absent adequate protection acceptable to CMG or ordered by the Court. Absent consent or adequate protection, the Debtor’s proposed use of Cash Collateral, which request conspicuously avoids addressing CMG’s substantial secured claim, violates section 363(c)(2) of the Bankruptcy Code and constitutes cause for the Court to prohibit or condition such use.

Accordingly, good cause exists for the entry of injunctive relief pursuant to sections 105(a) and 363(a) of the Bankruptcy Code.

### E. CMG Should Not Be Required to Post Security Under Federal Rule of Bankruptcy Procedure 7065

Because CMG seeks only to protect its Collateral and ensure the Debtor’s compliance with the Bankruptcy Code, no security should be required pursuant to Bankruptcy Rule 7065 and Rule 65(c) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. CMG seeks the relief requested herein as a direct result of the blatant failure of the Debtor to acknowledge CMG’s valid, perfected and first-priority security interest in the Collateral. The Debtor filed a voluntary petition and is subject to this Court’s jurisdiction, and the benefits and burdens of the Bankruptcy Code, including under section 363. The harm to the Defendants as a result of any preliminary injunctive relief would be minimal, if any harm exists at all. No security bond is appropriate.
