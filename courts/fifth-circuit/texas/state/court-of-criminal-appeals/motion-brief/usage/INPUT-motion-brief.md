# BASIC FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

## I. The Underlying Case

Robert Roberson’s daughter Nikki experienced chronic health challenges in her 27 months of life, including a documented history of antibiotic-resistant infections and breathing apnea when she would inexplicably collapse, cease breathing, turn blue, and have to be revived. In the last week of her life, Nikki suffered from a respiratory infection, vomiting, and diarrhea. Her father took her to the emergency room on January 28, 2002. The doctor in the county’s only hospital prescribed Phenergan in suppository form.[^2] Then Nikki developed a high fever; so, the next morning, her father took her to her pediatrician’s office where her temperature was measured at 104.5 degrees. The doctor diagnosed a “respiratory illness, likely viral” —but prescribed more Phenergan, this time combined with codeine. The FDA now restricts administering Phenergan and codeine to children because these drugs can cause breathing difficulties and death.

The next day, January 30, 2002, around 10:00 PM, Robert retrieved Nikki from her maternal grandparents’ house at their request. Although the grandparents had agreed to watch Nikki for a few days while Robert’s live-in girlfriend was in the hospital, they claimed that Mrs. Bowman (Nikki’s step-grandmother) had become ill too. Robert willingly drove out to their house in the country, took Nikki home, got her something to eat, and put her to bed.

In the early morning of January 31, 2002, Robert was awoken by a strange cry and found Nikki on the floor at the foot of the bed, seemingly after falling in her sleep. After comforting Nikki, the two eventually fell back asleep. But when Robert awoke later that morning, he found Nikki unconscious with blue lips.

Robert tried to revive Nikki then rushed to the local ER. But her eyes were already fixed and dilated (a grave sign). Despite efforts to reverse Nikki’s condition, she was removed from life support in Dallas on February 1, 2002, after being transferred to the Children’s Medical Center of Dallas (CMCD), where a child abuse pediatrician (CAP) diagnosed SBS based on Nikki’s triad of internal head conditions: (1) bleeding under the dura membrane outside of the brain (aka subdural hematoma); (2) brain swelling; and (3) retinal hemorrhages. CAP Janet Squires’ SBS diagnosis was used to obtain an arrest warrant for Robert moments after Nikki, who had been taken off life support without Robert’s knowledge or consent, was pronounced dead. A hasty autopsy was opened and closed the next morning. The same Palestine detective who had arrested Robert the night before sat in on the autopsy after conveying to the medical examiner that Nikki’s father had already been arrested for murder.

In 2002-2003, when Robert was accused, tried, and convicted, the medical consensus permitted presuming that a child had sustained inflicted head trauma whenever three internal head conditions were found (subdural hematoma, brain swelling, retinal hemorrhage). The assumption was that, where one or more of these symptoms were present, a child must have been the victim of intentionally inflicted abuse involving shaking and perhaps blunt impact and that whoever had been caring for that child when the symptoms became manifest must have been the culprit because there could be no “lucid interval.” Per Dr. Squires and other SBS proponents, absent some verified major trauma, such as a car wreck or a fall from a multistory building, inflicted trauma could be presumed.

Moreover, the medical community then mistakenly believed that short falls, like the one Robert had described, could never cause severe injuries. Based on the presumption that the triad was indicative of abuse, and the belief that short falls could not explain any aspect of Nikki’s condition, no other explanation was even considered. Additionally, as CAP Janet Squires noted in making her diagnosis, there did not seem to be any other explanation because Nikki’s maternal grandparents had provided Dr. Squires with the incorrect information that Nikki had been “totally well” the night before her collapse.[^3] Additionally, CMCD staff observed no injuries to the neck, chest, abdomen, genitalia, extremities, or posterior but only one minor abrasion on Nikki’s chin and one minor bump on the back of her head with no corresponding fracture of any kind. For Dr. Squires, this absence of external injury was further evidence that Nikki’s internal condition could only be explained by shaking.

No one considered Nikki’s respiratory illness, investigated her compromised lungs or the respiratory-suppressing medications she had been prescribed, or understood at that time how breathing difficulties can cause bleeding outside of the brain and brain swelling when a disease like pneumonia progresses to the point of sepsis (something that is now recognized by contemporary science).

The SBS hypothesis used to convict Robert has since been entirely discredited by evidence-based science. Today, the medical consensus is that many naturally occurring phenomena, such as Nikki’s pneumonia, can cause the triad of intracranial conditions. Today, even those who still believe that shaking, with or without impact, can produce the triad have accepted that a differential diagnosis is required and that abuse can only be a diagnosis of exclusion. That is, today, the medical consensus is that all accidental and natural conditions must be excluded as possible causes of the triad before abuse or inflicted trauma can be posited. In short, support for the SBS measured at 104.5 degrees, and that they had in their possession her prescriptions for Phenergan suppositories, Phenergan and codeine cough syrup, and Imodium. principles that formed the basis of Robert’s prosecution have eroded entirely. Yet the evidence he has amassed of (1) the changes in scientific understanding and (2) other causes of Nikki’s medical condition have yet to be considered by any court.

# GROUNDS FOR A STAY

New grounds argue strongly in favor of staying Robert Roberson’s October 16th execution date.

## I. No Law Required Setting the October-16th Execution Date, Which Merely Puts Untoward Pressure on this Court to Rush Adjudication of Pending Claims.

When the State sought the October-16th execution date, its motion all but ignored the fact that Mr. Roberson currently has pleadings pending before a higher court—i.e., this Court. The pending appeals are based on voluminous new evidence only available since this Court last considered his quest for habeas relief. Specifically, Mr. Roberson has a pending subsequent state habeas application pursuant to both Texas Code of Criminal Procedure Article 11.071, section 5(a) and Article 11.073 (“Subsequent Application”). The Subsequent Application is supported, inter alia, by an affidavit from a nationally recognized pathologist with expertise in bleeding disorders, Dr. Michael Laposata. Dr. Laposata has concluded that infection (Nikki’s undiagnosed pneumonia) and a serious bleeding disorder made Mr. Roberson’s daughter Nikki highly susceptible to internal bleeding and bruising; this condition, not any physical trauma, explains the minor bruising and the internal bleeding observed during Nikki’s 2002 autopsy. Mr. Roberson’s pending Subsequent Application is also supported by the Joint Statement of ten independent pathologists explaining why the conclusions of the medical examiner who performed the 2002 autopsy are deeply flawed and unreliable. The new evidence in the Subsequent Application supports these new claims:

• Claim 1: Roberson Is Entitled to Habeas Relief Because He Is Actually Innocent;

• Claim 2: Roberson Is Entitled to Habeas Relief under Article 11.073;

• Claim 3: Roberson has Been Deprived of a State-Created Liberty Interest in Violation of Federal Constitutional Law.

Mr. Roberson has also asked this Court to reconsider its previous decisions in his habeas proceedings based on Ex parte Roark, 707 S.W.3d 157 (Tex. Crim. App. 2024). Ex parte Roark was decided mere days before Mr. Roberson’s last scheduled execution date. In Ex parte Roark, the Court granted habeas relief under Article 11.073 based on changes in the scientific understanding of the same SBS hypothesis used to convict Mr. Roberson. (And Ex parte Roark involved the exact same SBS expert the State had used in Mr. Roberson’s 2003 trial.) Now that the Court has recognized the serious flaws underpinning the SBS hypothesis of that era, there is a compelling reason to revisit the decision to deny Mr. Roberson a new trial based on the habeas court’s erroneous FFCL that the SBS hypothesis had not changed since 2002-2003.[^9]

Article 43.141 of the Texas Code of Criminal Procedure contains the law relevant to setting execution dates. There is no provision in Texas law requiring a convicting court to set an execution date at a particular time. Article 43.141 contains only limitations on when an execution date can be set and identifies circumstances when a previously set execution date may be modified or withdrawn:

The convicting court may modify or withdraw the order of the court

setting a date for execution in a death penalty case if the court

determines that additional proceedings are necessary on: (1) a subsequent or untimely application for a writ of habeas

corpus filed under Article 11.071; or (2) a motion for forensic testing of DNA evidence submitted

under Chapter 64. T.CC.P. art. 43.141(d). Yet the newly assigned judge in the convicting court seems to have believed he had no choice but to set an execution date: “it doesn’t seem like anything is going to get resolved without a date to get folks to move, and that’s unfortunate, but it’s a reality of our system sometimes.” EX2 at 18.

Article 43.141(d) expressly grants a convicting court the discretion to withdraw an execution date after a subsequent habeas application has been filed. See also EX3 (samples of orders trial courts have entered withdrawing execution dates). Therefore, basic logic would suggest that it is neither required nor desirable to set an execution date when a subsequent habeas application is already pending. Indeed, it is highly unusual to seek an execution date under such circumstances. Therefore, during the July-16th hearing, Mr. Roberson’s counsel urged the convicting court to decline to set an execution date precisely because of the pending Subsequent Application. See EX2. There was no legal or practical reason to put pressure on this Court to short-circuit the adjudicative process when an execution date could easily be sought if this Court were to deny relief.

Article 11.071, section 5 provides that this Court has sole authority to determine whether to authorize any claim in a subsequent application for further development. Thus, when a subsequent application is pending and no execution date has already been scheduled, a convicting court should not schedule an execution if additional proceedings are necessary. T.CC.P. art. 43.141(d). Since only this Court is authorized to determine whether additional proceedings are necessary on a subsequent application and because Article 43.141(d)(1) expressly provides for withdrawing an execution date after a subsequent habeas application is filed, this Court should grant the instant Emergency Motion for Stay of Execution. Then this Court can continue with the adjudicative process without the ticking timebomb of an imminent execution date.

The convicting court’s rationale for setting an execution date at this time was expressed as the need to “get folks to move.” EX2 at 18. But there is no statute or caselaw suggesting that putting pressure on a higher court to issue a decision is a valid basis for setting an execution date. The need for full and fair deliberation should be especially obvious in a case that has been fraught with due process problems and where considerable evidence of innocence has been amassed but not yet considered. See 06 Application.

The State will sustain no harm if its quest for an execution date is temporarily delayed or permanently prevented, as the State has no legitimate interest in executing an innocent person. By contrast, Mr. Roberson will experience irreparable harm if a stay is not granted and the merits of his pending claims are never fully or fairly considered.

Finally, the public interest is not served by rushing this Court’s adjudication. The public has no interest in seeing potentially innocent people executed. By contrast, the fair and accurate resolution of this case will serve the public interest.

## II. Significant Changes in Scientific Understanding and in New Evidence of Innocence Have Been Adduced Since this Court Last Considered the Merits of Applicant’s Claims.

The merits of Mr. Roberson’s Actual Innocence and Article 11.073 claims have not been considered since the 03 proceeding, where evidence was closed in March of 2021. In the habeas court’s FFCL that issued nearly a year later, there is no mention of most of the evidence that had been amassed during the 03 proceeding—as if Mr. Roberson had not presented six highly qualified experts or substantial evidence of how the SBS hypothesis, circa 2002-2003, had been completely debunked. There were no adverse credibility findings, only a complete disregard of the habeas record that had been built. But then this Court expressly relied on those misleading FFCL when it denied relief on January 11, 2023, the culmination of serious due process violations. See 06 Application, Claim 3.

Since then, a great deal of additional evidence of Mr. Roberson’s innocence and of problems with the SBS hypothesis has been amassed. But no court has yet considered that evidence. All subsequent applications have been dismissed without considering the merits.

Critically, when a habeas applicant brings an Actual Innocence claim in a subsequent application, there is no requirement that he prove that his new evidence of innocence was not “previously available,” as is true with other kinds of claims. T.C.C.P. art. 11.071 sec. 5 (a)(1). Texas law requires only that the habeas applicant allege sufficient facts to show that, “by a preponderance of the evidence, but for a violation of the United States Constitution no rational juror could have found the applicant guilty beyond a reasonable doubt.” See id. sec. 5 (a)(2). Thus, in deciding the threshold issue of whether Mr. Roberson’s pending Actual Innocence claim is procedurally barred, this Court must consider all of the evidence, old and new, and decide whether, under the “easy to meet” preponderance standard, any “rational juror could have found the applicant guilty beyond a reasonable doubt.” Id. See also Ex parte Cobb, 710 S.W.3d 747 (Tex. Crim. App. 2025) (Yeary. J.) (describing the “preponderance of the evidence standard” as “easy to meet”). When this Court applies the appropriate standard to the evidence, old and new, it will conclude that no rational juror could find Mr. Roberson guilty beyond a reasonable doubt since the reasons to doubt his guilt are overwhelming.

Of course, for a habeas applicant to ultimately prevail on an Actual Innocence claim, this Court “must be convinced that new facts ‘unquestionably establish’ applicant’s innocence;” and the term “unquestionably establish” means to establish by “clear and convincing evidence.” Ex parte Elizondo, 947 S.W.2d 202, 209 (Tex. Crim. App. 1996). But Article 11.071, section 5 imposes a much lower threshold burden for an Actual Innocence claim to be authorized; and Mr. Roberson has easily satisfied that threshold burden, which also means he has satisfied the “easy” preponderance standard for obtaining relief under Article 11.073.

### A. The SBS testimony in the Roark and Roberson cases was virtually identical, a circumstance that has yet to be addressed in any court decision.

This Court granted habeas relief to Andrew Roark following consideration that commenced nearly 11 months after Mr. Roberson had been denied relief in the only habeas proceeding in which his claims were ever considered on the merits— but where all of his robust evidence of innocence was simply ignored by the habeas judge in flagrant violation of his right to due process.

Mr. Roberson’s case involves numerous overlapping issues with those raised in Mr. Roark’s case—which Mr. Roberson flagged for the habeas judge before she issued her FFCL, a fact that was also ignored. Additionally, the FFCL did not mention any of the evidence of “changed science” that Mr. Roberson, like Mr. Roark, had introduced into the habeas record.

Both convictions (of Mr. Roark and of Mr. Roberson) hinged on the same version of the SBS hypothesis. Both cases were tried in the same era (2000 and 2003, respectively) when a version of SBS, now universally rejected, was viewed as medical orthodoxy; and both trials featured the very same CAP, Dr. Squires.

In both cases, Dr. Squires opined that three medical findings—(i) subdural bleeding, (ii) cerebral edema aka brain swelling, and (iii) retinal hemorrhage— permitted presuming that abusive shaking, perhaps with blunt impact, had occurred.

The version of SBS put before Mr. Roark’s and Mr. Roberson’s juries, which permitted presuming inflicted head trauma, has since been rejected—because numerous other phenomena, including accidental short falls with head impact and naturally occurring disease, such as pneumonia, can cause the same constellation of intracranial symptoms.[^10] Likewise, the presumption, embraced by Dr. Squires and other SBS-proponents at that time, that the adult with a child when she became symptomatic had to be the “perpetrator” of inflicted head injury because there could be no “lucid interval,” has also been proven false. The cascade of symptoms— subdural bleeding, brain swelling, retinal hemorrhages—can develop over many hours or even days before reaching a crisis point when a child collapses, seemingly for no reason.

Although the Roark case involved a 13-month-old who sustained brain damage but lived, and Mr. Roberson’s daughter Nikki was a 27-month-old who died, much of Dr. Squires’ trial testimony speculating about the cause of each child’s condition is virtually identical—and none of it would withstand scrutiny today.[^11] Her testimony described the violent shaking she imagined had occurred, with the child’s head flopping back and forth after which the child was, perhaps, flung down, and left with a massive brain injury. This gruesome, unchallenged hypothetical had to have profoundly affected Mr. Roberson’s and Mr. Roark’s juries. It was presented to both juries as established fact: that a doctor could look at the child’s internal head condition and posit that abuse had occurred without further investigation.

But today, while everyone agrees that violently shaking an infant is horrible, no study has ever shown that shaking can cause any of the internal head conditions associated with the SBS hypothesis (subdural hematoma, brain swelling, retinal hemorrhage). For instance, a study published in 2020 evaluated 36 infants subjected to either admitted or witnessed shaking (with or without blunt force head impact). Of these infants, none who had been purportedly shaken exhibited any element of the triad. See I. Thiblin et al., Medical Findings and Symptoms in Infants Exposed to Witnessed or Admitted Abusive Shaking: A Nationwide Registry Study, 15 PLoS One 8–9 (2020). It is well understood today that shaking, if it causes physical injury, would be experienced first and foremost in a child’s neck. (Mr. Roberson’s daughter Nikki had no neck injuries of any kind.) extensive school, military, child custody, and TDCJ records do not include any instances of violent behavior. See 7EHRR128-129. Although Dr. Squires and others repeatedly referred to Nikki as a “baby,” she was in fact a 27-month-old toddler who weighed nearly 30 pounds; toddlers’ necks and brains are markedly different from infants’. But the identical, now discredited tenets of SBS were applied to the condition of the 13-month-old in Roark’s case and to the 27-month-old toddler in Roberson’s case as if these two children were anatomically and medically indistinguishable, an approach now seen as unscientific.

By 2016, when Mr. Roberson finally had counsel to raised his changedscience and innocence claims, the invalidity of the SBS hypothesis used to accuse and convict Mr. Roberson had become so obvious that, after his 03 Application was filed, the Anderson County DA’s Office and medical examiner Urban tried to distance themselves from SBS, claiming this was not really a shaken baby case but simply a case of “blunt force” head trauma.[^13] This argument, however, is belied by a trial record replete with descriptions of “shaking” from both of the State’s causation experts (Dr. Janet Squires and medical examiner Dr. Jill Urban). The State’s trial experts also used words like “rotational forces” and “shearing,” biomechanical terms commonly used in SBS cases. No objective reader of the trial transcripts could fail to see that both the Roark and Roberson cases were “classic” SBS cases in the worst sense: cases presented to the jury as if they were grounded in science when we now know there is no underlying science to support the testimony that the jury heard. Even the most vocal proponents of SBS/AHT, like the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), recognize that medical understanding has evolved considerably since 2003—such that there is no legitimate debate that naturally occurring diseases, like pneumonia, and accidental short falls with head impact, can produce the same intracranial symptoms previously (and incorrectly) viewed as “diagnostic” of shaking and abuse.

The Roark case emphasized five principles that are defined as “false testimony,” four of which are also part of the false testimony put before Mr. Roberson’s jury.[^14] Those false tenets are: • Short distance falls cannot cause subdural hematomas. • Shaking alone can cause this injury. • There cannot be a lucid injury after a child is injured. • Retinal bleeding is proof of shaking.

Both Mr. Roark and Mr. Roberson showed how significant problems with the tenets of SBS started to reach the larger medical community, such that the AAP, in 2009, abandoned the SBS name and switched to the undefined term “Abusive Head Trauma” aka “AHT.” The name change, however, did not resolve the bigger problem that became increasingly evident over time: no evidence-based research supports the SBS/AHT hypothesis.[^15]
