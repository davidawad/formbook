# I. Nature of the proceedings

After the defendant, Robert Nichols, repeatedly slammed the minor victim against the floor and separately struck his head against a countertop, the State charged the defendant with one count of assault in the first degree, [General Statutes § 53a-59(a)(3)](toa:cgs-53a-59); and two counts of risk of injury; [General Statutes § 53-21(a)(1)](toa:cgs-53-21). Clerk Appendix (C.App.), 10-11. Following a trial, on June 26, 2023, a jury found the defendant guilty as charged. Id., 8, 24. Thereafter, the trial court imposed a total effective sentence of 20 years of incarceration, execution suspended after 10 years, followed by 5 years of probation. Id., 8, 24.

# II. Counterstatement of the facts

From the evidence presented at trial, and the inferences reasonably derived therefrom, the jury could have found the following:

The defendant was the pastor of a small congregation of six to ten people. T.6/20/23: 54-55. He and his wife also operated a daycare out of their Manchester home. Id., 64, 223.

Congregants of the defendant's church included DG, RG, and DH.[^1] Id., 55. When they joined the church, DG and RG were a dating couple. Id., 56-57, 195-99. Approximately one year after joining the church, DG and RG married, and the defendant performed the ceremony. Id., 57, 196. Thereafter, the defendant conducted marital counseling sessions with them. Id., 59-62, 197-99. He also encouraged them to sever ties with their families. Id., 62, 199.

In October 2012, RG gave birth to a son, the victim. Id., 63, 200. At that time, both DG and RG were working, but were not making a substantial amount of money. Id., 63-64, 200. Therefore, they enrolled the victim in the defendant's daycare. Id., 64, 201. On the application form, the emergency contacts, other than DG and RG, were the defendant, the defendant's wife, and DH–all of whom worked at the daycare. Id., 66-68, 201, 203; State's Exhibit (St.Ex.) 1 (daycare form). The form also listed DH and the defendant's wife as individuals to whom their son could be released from the daycare. T.6/20/23: 67-68. DG and RG paid for the defendant's daycare services either with money or by cleaning the defendant's home and receiving a credit toward the daycare costs. Id., 68-69, 201. During this time, the defendant and his wife were very controlling regarding how DG and RG provided for the victim, including reprimanding RG for the brand of yogurt that she fed him, the clothing he wore, and for continuing to breastfeed him and store breast milk for him. Id., 70-72.

The defendant and his wife sometimes watched the victim outside of the normal daycare hours. Id., 72. It was not uncommon for them to ask to keep the victim overnight. Id., 72. RG did not feel as though she could refuse these requests. Id., 72-73.

In August 2013, while RG was at work, she received a call asking her to pick up DG at his workplace because he was sick with a stomach ailment. Id., 73. At that time, the victim was at the defendant's daycare. Id. The defendant or his wife advised RG to take DG home, disinfect their residence, and pick up the victim after RG's illness had passed. Id. RG, however, also fell ill and the victim remained at the daycare for approximately ten days.[^2] Id., 73-75.

During this approximate ten-day period, DG and RG recovered from their illnesses and attended a service at the defendant's church. Id., 74. They anticipated seeing the victim at that time, but the defendant did not allow them to do so. Id. He told them that they were not going to get the victim back at that time because the victim was "rebellious." Id., 74, 204-05, 227-28. The defendant told RG that the victim's "rebellious behavior was because of [her], because [she] was rebellious, and [she] had passed that onto [her] son." Id., 75, 152-53, 205.

At that time, DH was living at the defendant's home and working for the daycare and as a personal assistant to the defendant and his wife in order to pay off debts that she owed them. T.6/21/23: 100-02, 122-25. The defendant and his wife had, in DH's eyes, become her spiritual parents to such an extent that she had begun calling them "dad" and "mom." Id., 109-10. They had driven a wedge between DH and her biological parents and, eventually, had adopted her as an adult. Id., 112-13, 121. DH also was engaging in sexual activity with the defendant and his wife. Id., 122.

Shortly after the victim began staying at the defendant's home, DH saw the defendant put the victim in a sink filled with cold water and ice cubes. Id., 129. He also used a nozzle from the sink to spray water over the victim's head. Id. This caused the victim to flail about, gasp for air, and fall backward and bang his head on a granite countertop. Id., 129-30. DH recalled that the defendant had inflicted this as punishment because the nine-month-old victim was failing to follow the defendant's directions as he was trying to teach the victim how to walk. Id., 130. DH was told that the victim was being rebellious because of RG and had to be disciplined. Id., 130. The defendant said that, "if he can't breathe, if he's fighting for his life, he can do it." Id.

DH also saw the defendant slap and hit the victim until he had bruises on his legs. Id., 130-31. She also observed the defendant swaddle the victim by forcefully putting him into a Pack n' Play with pillows on either side of him that forced him into an upright position. Id., 131. When the defendant forced the victim into the Pack n' Play, DH heard a "thud." Id., 131. The defendant then dragged the victim by his legs and left a rug burn on his head. Id., 131, 133-34. He also slammed the victim on the floor while he was swaddled three or four times with "a loud thud sound." Id., 131. DH recalled that these incidents occurred early during the victim's ten-day stay at the defendant's home. Id., 132.

After these events, the victim had bruises and marks on his head. Id. The defendant was concerned that RG would see the bruises, not "understand how necessary this was," and call a doctor. Id. Therefore, he decided to keep the victim at his home until the bruises healed. Id. The defendant also did not allow DH to take any photographs of the victim after the bruises appeared. Id., 132-33.

After approximately ten days, the defendant returned the victim to DG and RG. T.6/20/23: 75, 149-50. At that time, RG observed that the victim "was not as happy as he normally was." Id., 75. While he previously had been a "happy, jovial baby," he did not "seem himself" and had "lost the light that was in his eyes." Id. She also noted that the victim was vomiting periodically–more frequently than before his stay at the daycare–and he appeared paler than normal. Id., 75, 119-20. Further, she observed that the victim had bruises on his legs. Id., 153-55. She also noted that the victim would cry whenever she would give him to the defendant. Id., 160, 173. DG likewise observed that the victim's color looked different and he appeared "somber" and malnourished. Id., 205-06, 231.

RG wanted to take the victim to the hospital and had asked DG if she could do so. Id., 75-76. DG, however, told her that she could not do so and took the car keys from her. Id. RG felt that she had to obey her husband.[^3] Id., 76-77.

In September 2013, DG, RG, and the victim moved out of their apartment and into the defendant's basement. Id., 77, 207-08. The defendant and his wife had proposed the move as an opportunity for DG and RG to have more time with them and to be able to hear God's voice. Id., 79. They also presented it as a way for DG and RG to save money. Id. DG's sister, KC, helped DG and RG pack up their apartment for the move.[^4] T.6/21/23: 63. At that time, KC observed that, although the victim was acting "fine," he appeared to be cross-eyed. Id., 64, 67-68. KC also observed RG telling the victim to put his head up. Id., 64-65, 69.

When DG, RG, and the victim moved into the defendant's home, DH already was living there with the defendant and his wife. T.6/30/23: 80-81, 209. Over time, the relationship between the defendant and his wife and DG and RG became so close that, like DH, DG began calling the defendant and his wife "dad" and "mom." Id., 223-24.

After DG, RG, and the victim moved into the defendant's home, they all went on a trip with the defendant, his wife, and DH to Florida, where the defendant and his wife had a timeshare. Id., 81-82. While DG, RG, and DH traveled to Florida by plane, the defendant and his wife drove the victim there in their car. Id., 81, 210-12. On the return trip, however, the victim traveled with DG and RG on a plane. Id., 82. RG recalled that, by the time of the Florida trip, the victim's symptoms had begun to subside and he was returning to his normal self. Id., 155-56.

On October 9, 2013, DG and RG brought the victim to a pediatrician for a routine wellness visit. Id., 82-83, 213. During the visit, the pediatrician advised them to schedule an ultrasound examination for the victim because he had an enlarged head. Id., 84; T.6/21/23: 30. The victim underwent an ultrasound several days later, which revealed pockets of fluid on both sides of his brain.[^5] T.6/20/23: 84-85, 213-14; T.6/21/23: 31. Thereafter, the hospital asked DG and RG to bring the victim back to the hospital for an MRI. T.6/20/23: 85, 214.

The defendant was present when DG and RG received the call asking them to schedule the MRI. Id., 86. The defendant directed DG and RG to pray over the victim and to believe that the fluid would dissipate. Id., 214; T.6/21/23: 34. He also advised them to contact their insurance provider to confirm that their insurance would cover an MRI. T.6/20/23: 86. They did so and learned that their insurance would not cover an MRI without a referral. Id., 86, 214, 237. DG and RG then called their pediatrician, but the pediatrician refused to provide a referral. Id., 86. DG and RG, therefore, never scheduled an MRI. Id., 85-86. RG then left and went to work. Id., 86.

Later that evening, an ambulance and police arrived at the defendant's home and transported the victim and DG to the hospital.[^6] Id., 215. The defendant also went to the hospital. Id., 216. When RG returned from work, the defendant and his wife informed her that the victim had been taken to the hospital. Id., 87, 145. They advised RG not to go to the hospital because she "wasn't able to handle it" and that DG would remain at the hospital. Id., 87. The victim then stayed at the hospital for several days before the defendant and his wife gave RG permission to go there. Id., 87, 145, 235. While at the hospital, the victim underwent an MRI.[^7] T.6/22/23: 26.

The defendant advised RG that a detective or someone from the hospital might try to "corner" her and ask if she was "safe at home and stuff like that." T.6/20/23: 88. He directed RG not to speak to anyone without her husband present and told DG and RG what to say if anyone spoke to them. Id., 88, 93, 217.

RG spoke to a detective on October 15, 2013, with DG present. T.6/20/23: 87-88, 93. RG provided a statement, in which she said that she "[did] not know of anything that could have caused a serious injury to [her] son." Id., 94-95, 137-40; Defendant's Exhibit (Def.Ex.) A. In the statement, RG did not tell police that the victim had substantially changed when he returned to her following his stay at the defendant's home in 2013. T.6/20/23: 138; Def.Ex. A. She signed the statement, which included an affirmation that the statement was true to the best of her knowledge. T.6/20/23: 140; Def.Ex. A. She also affirmed that the statement was voluntary and that no one had forced her to make the statements. T.6/20/23: 164; Def.Ex. A. DG likewise provided a statement on that same date in which he similarly did not mention the victim's symptoms following his stay at the defendant's home, and did not say that he suspected that the defendant may have abused the victim. T.6/20/23: 239-41; T.6/21/23: 3-4; Def.Ex. D. DH also provided a statement to police at that time in which she stated that she never had seen anything abnormal in the victim's behavior, that she had neither caused nor seen any bumps or injuries to his head, and that she had not seen the victim act in any way that made her think he had been abused. T.6/21/23: 162; Def.Ex. H.

One day later, on October 16, 2013, RG provided a second statement, in which she proffered excuses for the victim's injuries, including that he may have been injured when RG pulled him away from a large wave at the beach in Florida in early October 2013. T.6/20/23: 94-95, 141-43, 218; T.6/21/23: 19; Def.Ex. B. Alternatively, RG suggested that she may have injured the victim when she fell while removing him from his car seat and he struck his head against the car's roof. T.6/20/23: 142-43; Def.Ex. B. DG also suggested that the victim may have rolled off the bed at their apartment. T.6/20/23: 218; T.6/21/23: 19. RG also advised that she did not feed the victim cow's milk and only recently had realized that the victim might not have been receiving recommended levels of calcium. T.6/20/23: 143; Def.Ex. B. These excuses were ones that the defendant had directed RG to provide. T.6/20/23: 94-95, 143, 177-78. As with her first statement, RG signed the second statement, which also included an affirmation that the statement was true to the best of her knowledge.[^8] T.6/20/23: 144; Def.Ex. B. She also again affirmed that her statement was voluntary and that she had not been forced to make the statements. T.6/20/23: 164; Def.Ex. B.

The defendant himself also spoke to police on October 16, 2013. T.6/23/23: 22-23. When questioned about possible causes for the victim's injury, he described one time when the victim had been sitting in the sink during a bath and had bumped the edge of the sink. Id.; T.6/22/23: 31. The defendant reported that the victim had cried briefly, but seemed fine and uninjured. T.6/22/23: 31; T.6/23/23: 23. The defendant said that the incident had been so minor that he had not mentioned it to the victim's parents. T.6/22/23: 31; T.6/23/23: 22-23. The defendant also reported that RG had suggested that the injury may have occurred at the beach when a wave struck the victim, or when RG banged his head while taking him out of a car. T.6/23/23: 23-24; St.Ex. 10.

Subsequently, in November 2013, RG wrote a letter in support of the defendant and his wife maintaining their daycare license at a time when their daycare was under investigation. T.6/20/23: 146-47, 174; Def.Ex. C. In that letter, RG did not state that she suspected that the victim had been injured while at the defendant's daycare; rather, she opined that the daycare provided "good care." T.6/20/23: 146-47, 174. RG wrote the letter following a conversation with the defendant and his wife. Id., 147. The defendant, his wife, and DG had instructed RG to write the letter. Id., 147, 174. DG also wrote a letter at the same time, in which he asserted that the did not hold the defendant, his wife, or DH responsible for any accusations of child abuse or neglect toward the victim, and that DG was content with the victim continuing to attend the defendant's daycare. T.6/21/23: 6-7; Def.Ex. E.

DG and RG ultimately lost custody of the victim following his injuries, and he was placed in foster care by the Department of Children and Families. T.6.20/23: 95-96, 162-63, 219. RG, however, was able to see the victim during visits and medical appointments. Id., 96-97. Following a conversation with the victim's foster mother, RG came to believe that if she and DG moved out of the defendant's basement, it could increase her chances of getting her son back. Id., 99. When RG informed the defendant and his wife of her belief, the defendant told her that "it was a distraction to take [them] off of God's plan." Id., 100. The defendant advised her to disregard the thought and accused her of being disloyal to him. Id. He further stated that RG had "opened the door to Satan and that was why [the victim] was injured in the first place." Id. The defendant blamed RG's "rebellious spirit" for the victim's injury. Id., 101.

Eventually, though, DG and RG decided to leave the defendant's church and move out of his home. Id., 101-02. RG relocated to Berlin, joined another church, and, in June 2014, regained custody of the victim. Id., 102. RG also subsequently obtained a divorce from DG. Id., 103-04. DG also moved out of the defendant's home in 2016. T.6/21/23: 8-9, 143. At that time, DG wrote a letter to the defendant and his wife thanking them for their hospitality. Id.; Def. Ex. F.

Two years later, in 2018, DH contacted RG and told her about the abuse that she had observed the defendant inflict on the victim nearly five years earlier in 2013. T.6/21/23: 194-95. At that time, RG advised DH that the statute of limitation had not yet run. Id., 195. Upon hearing this, DH decided to give another statement to police. Id.

Thereafter, on June 4, 2018, RG provided a third statement, in which she conveyed different information than she had in her 2013 statements because, at that time in 2018, she felt that she was able to speak for herself.[^9] T.6/20/23: 103-04, 164-65; St.Ex. 4. Therein, RG recounted how she and DG had fallen ill in August 2013, and how the defendant and his wife had taken the victim and not allowed RG and DG to see him for approximately ten days. T.6/20/23: 166; St.Ex. 4. RG reported that the defendant and his wife said that the victim was "being rebellious and offered to help break him of his bad behavior." T.6/20/23: 166; St.Ex. 4. She further noted that, when the defendant eventually returned the victim to them, she immediately knew that something was wrong with him, "[i]t was like a light went off," and "[h]e was not as happy or laughing." T.6/20/23: 166; St.Ex. 4. She recounted how, for a few weeks thereafter, the victim had difficulty keeping his head up and was vomiting frequently, which was unusual. T.6/20/23: 166; St.Ex. 4. RG asserted that "[a]ll of this happened in August of 2013." T.6/20/23: 166; St.Ex. 4.

### 2. Standard of review and relevant legal principles

The standard of review applicable on appellate review of challenges to the sufficiency of the evidence is well established:

> In reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence to support a criminal conviction [this Court] appl[ies] a two-part test. First, [it] construe[s] the evidence in the light most favorable to sustaining the verdict. Second, [it] determine[s] whether upon the facts so construed and the inferences reasonably drawn therefrom the finder of fact reasonably could have concluded that the cumulative force of the evidence established guilt beyond a reasonable doubt….

(Brackets in original omitted.) [State v. Na'im B., 288 Conn. 290, 295-96, 952 A.2d 755 (2008)](toa:naim-b); accord [State v. Hughes, 341 Conn. 387, 397-98, 267 A.3d 81 (2021)](toa:hughes); see [Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 318-19, 99 S. Ct. 2781, 61 L. Ed. 2d 560 (1979)](toa:jackson). "This familiar standard gives full play to the responsibility of the trier of fact fairly to resolve conflicts in the testimony, to weigh the evidence, and to draw reasonable inferences from basic facts to ultimate facts." [State v. Stellato, 10 Conn. App. 447, 452, 523 A.2d 1345 (1987)](toa:stellato), quoting [Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 319](toa:jackson).

> [T]he jury must find every element proven beyond a reasonable doubt in order to find the defendant guilty of the charged offense, but each of the basic and inferred facts underlying those conclusions need not be proved beyond a reasonable doubt…. If it is reasonable and logical for the jury to conclude that a basic fact or an inferred fact is true, the jury is permitted to consider the fact proven and may consider it in combination with other proven facts in determining whether the cumulative effect of all the evidence proves the defendant guilty of all the elements of the crime charged beyond a reasonable doubt….

(Brackets in original omitted.) [State v. Na'im B., 288 Conn. 296](toa:naim-b); see also [State v. Niemeyer, 258 Conn. 510, 517-19, 782 A.2d 658 (2001)](toa:niemeyer) (explicating sufficiency principles). Due to these standards, "[a] party challenging the validity of the jury's verdict on grounds that there was insufficient evidence to support such a result carries a difficult burden." [State v. Hughes, 341 Conn. 397](toa:hughes).

### 3. The evidence was sufficient

The evidence was sufficient to sustain the defendant's convictions because, contrary to the defendant's arguments, DH's testimony alone was enough to sustain the charges. As part of the governing standard of review, this Court must presume that the jury credited DH's testimony, regardless of any impeachment presented. Viewed in the light most favorable to sustaining the verdict, DH's testimony established that the defendant placed the victim in and ice bath and sprayed him with cold water, thereby causing him to flail and strike his head against a granite countertop and, thereafter, banged the swaddled victim's head against the floor. Further, DH's testimony that the defendant's abuse of the victim occurred within the days following August 10, 2013, was sufficient to satisfy the statute of limitation.

The defendant's arguments that DH was incredible based upon his efforts to impeach her, and his implicit request that this Court should discredit her testimony, do not undermine the sufficiency of the evidence. Rather, "it is the function of the jury to consider the evidence and to judge the credibility of witnesses." (Brackets in original omitted.) [State v. Williams, 350 Conn. 363, 374, 324 A.3d 760 (2024)](toa:williams). It is well established that "[t]he credited testimony of even a single witness may be sufficient to sustain a defendant's conviction." [State v. Franklin, 162 Conn. App. 78, 89, 129 A.3d 770 (2015), cert. denied, 321 Conn. 905, 138 A.3d 281 (2016)](toa:franklin); see [State v. Whitaker, 215 Conn. 739, 756 n.18, 578 A.2d 1031 (1990)](toa:whitaker); [State v. Hodge, 153 Conn. 564, 572-73, 219 A.2d 367 (1966)](toa:hodge).

"The inquiry into whether the record evidence would support a finding of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt does not require a court to ask itself whether it believes that the evidence … established guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. … Instead, the relevant question is whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to sustaining the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." (Citation omitted; emphasis in original; quotation marks omitted.) [State v. Boykin, 27 Conn. App. 558, 563-64, 609 A.2d 242, cert. denied, 223 Conn. 905, 610 A.2d 179 (1992)](toa:boykin); see [Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 318-19](toa:jackson); see also [State v. Sivri, 231 Conn. 115, 135-36, 646 A.2d 169 (1994)](toa:sivri). Stated differently, when reviewing a sufficiency of the evidence claim, "[t]his [C]ourt does not retry the case or evaluate the credibility of the witnesses." (Quotation marks omitted.) [State v. McClam, 44 Conn. App. 198, 208, 689 A.2d 475, cert. denied, 240 Conn. 912, 690 A.2d 400 (1997)](toa:mcclam); accord [State v. Montana, 179 Conn. App. 261, 265, 178 A.3d 1119, cert. denied, 328 Conn. 911, 178 A.3d 1042 (2018)](toa:montana). "Rather, [this Court] must defer to the trier of fact's assessment of the credibility of the witnesses based on its firsthand observation of their conduct, demeanor, and attitude…." [State v. McClam, 44 Conn. App. 208](toa:mcclam); accord [State v. Juarez, 179 Conn. App. 588, 596, 180 A.3d 1015 (2018), cert. denied, 331 Conn. 910, 203 A.3d 1245 (2019)](toa:juarez); see [State v. Covington, 184 Conn. App. 332, 343, 194 A.3d 1224 (2018), aff'd, 335 Conn. 212, 229 A.3d 1036 (2020)](toa:covington-app).

The defendant's arguments in support of his sufficiency claim, which simply contend that the jury should not have credited DH because she was unbelievable, misconstrue and fail to apply the governing standard of review. See [State v. Jagat, 111 Conn. App. 173, 178-79, 958 A.2d 206 (2008)](toa:jagat); [State v. Mungroo, 104 Conn. App. 668, 670-73, 935 A.2d 229 (2007), cert. denied, 285 Conn. 908, 942 A.2d 415 (2008)](toa:mungroo). Under the standard of review, "[q]uestions of whether to believe or to disbelieve a competent witness are beyond [this Court's] review." [State v. Whitnum-Baker, 169 Conn. App. 523, 527, 150 A.3d 1174 (2016), cert. denied, 324 Conn. 923, 155 A.3d 753 (2017)](toa:whitnum-baker); accord [State v. Jagat, 111 Conn. App. 178](toa:jagat); [State v. Montoya, 110 Conn. App. 97, 104, 954 A.2d 193, cert. denied, 289 Conn. 941, 959 A.2d 1008 (2008)](toa:montoya).

In light of the governing standard, a claim that a witness' testimony was inconsistent or unreliable "does not challenge the sufficiency of the evidence." [State v. Carlos C., 165 Conn. App. 195, 200, 138 A.3d 1090, cert. denied, 322 Conn. 906, 140 A.3d 977 (2016)](toa:carlos-c). Rather, it asks this Court to "examine the credibility of the [witness], which [this Court is] unable to do." Id.

For these reasons, the defendant's claim that the jury should not have, or could not have, credited DH's testimony as to the defendant's injury of the victim or the timeframe in which it occurred is not truly a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence. At least, it does not sound under the governing standard of review. This Court must construe the record in the light most favorable to sustaining the verdict. That entails construing the record as though the jury credited DH's testimony in all respects.

DH testified that, within the days after August 10, 2013, she observed the defendant place the victim in an ice bath and spray cold water over his head causing him to flail about and strike his head on a granite countertop and, thereafter, swaddled him and banged his head on the floor. She further testified that, thereafter, she observed bruises and other injuries on the victim. Additionally, she testified that the defendant, thereafter, decided to keep the victim from his parents for ten days so that his bruises would heal and his mother would not see them. Subsequently, when medical personnel discovered the victim's subdural hematomas, the defendant scripted the answers that DG and RG should provide if questioned about how the victim had been injured. The defendant's efforts to conceal and obfuscate the causes of the victim's injury evinced a consciousness of guilt. Construed in the light most favorable to sustaining the verdict, DH's testimony in these respects was sufficient to prove the charges against the defendant.

This Court must presume that the jury either discredited the evidence that the defendant pressed in an effort to impeach her credibility, or that it otherwise did not afford that evidence much weight in its analysis. In turn this Court must presume that the jury credited DH's testimony supporting the verdicts and made factual findings consistent with those credibility determinations. So construed, the evidence was sufficient.

### 2. Standard of review and relevant legal principles

The standard of review applied on appellate review of a trial court's denial of a motion for a new trial premised on a claim that the verdict was against the weight of the evidence is well established:

> [A] challenge to the weight of the evidence is not the same as a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence. A sufficiency claim disputes that the state presented sufficient evidence, if found credible by the jury, to sustain the defendant's conviction…. In contrast, a weight claim does not contend that the state's evidence … was insufficient, as a matter of law, to establish the defendant's guilt beyond a reasonable doubt…. Rather, it asserts that the state's case … was so flimsy as to raise a substantial question regarding the reliability of the verdict and that there was a serious danger that the defendant was wrongly convicted….
>
> Given that these two types of claims raise fundamentally different issues, the inquiry appropriately undertaken by a court ruling on a sufficiency of the evidence claim differs substantially from that of a court ruling on a weight of the evidence claim. In reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence, a court considers whether there is a reasonable view of the evidence that would support a guilty verdict…. In doing so, the court does not sit as a seventh juror who may cast a vote against the verdict based upon [its] feeling that some doubt of guilt is shown by the cold printed record…. It cannot substitute its own judgment for that of the jury if there is sufficient evidence to support the jury's verdict…. Thus, a court will not reweigh the evidence or resolve questions of credibility in determining whether the evidence was sufficient….
>
> In contrast, a court determining if the verdict is against the weight of the evidence does precisely what a court ruling on a sufficiency claim ought not to do. That is, the court must do just what every juror ought to do in arriving at a verdict. The juror must use all his experience, his knowledge of human nature, his knowledge of human events, past and present, his knowledge of the motives which influence and control human action, and test the evidence in the case according to such knowledge and render his verdict accordingly…. The trial judge in considering the verdict must do the same … and if, in the exercise of all his knowledge from this source, he finds the verdict to be clearly against the weight of the evidence in the case as to indicate that the jury did not correctly apply the law to the facts in evidence in the case, or were governed by ignorance, prejudice, corruption or partiality, then it is his duty to set aside that verdict and to grant a new trial…. In other words, the court specifically is required to act as a seventh juror because it must independently assess the credibility of witnesses and determine the weight that should be given to … evidence….
>
> Thus, because a court is required to independently assess credibility and assign weight to evidence, a weight of the evidence claim necessarily raises the issue of which courts are competent to perform those tasks. It is well settled that only the judge who presided over the trial where a challenged verdict was returned is legally competent to decide if that verdict was against the weight of the evidence…. Consequently, a judge in a later proceeding, such as a direct appeal or a habeas corpus proceeding, is not legally competent to decide such a claim on the basis of the cold printed record before it…. The rationale behind this rule is sound: The trial court is uniquely situated to entertain a motion to set aside a verdict as against the weight of the evidence because, unlike an appellate court, the trial court has the same opportunity as the jury to view the witnesses, to assess their credibility and to determine the weight that should be given to their evidence…. The trial judge can gauge the tenor of the trial, as an appellate court, on the written record, cannot, and can detect those factors, if any, that could improperly have influenced the jury.

(Brackets in original omitted; emphasis in original; quotation marks omitted.) [State v. Capasso, 203 Conn. App. 333, 345-47, 248 A.3d 58, cert. denied, 336 Conn. 939, 249 A.3d 352 (2021)](toa:capasso); see [State v. Fred C., 167 Conn. App. 600, 606-07, 142 A.3d 1258, cert. denied, 323 Conn. 921, 150 A.3d 1150 (2016)](toa:fred-c).

It necessarily follows that appellate review of a weight of the evidence claims is greatly circumscribed. … The proper appellate standard of review when considering the action of a trial court granting or denying a motion to set aside a verdict and a motion for a new trial is the abuse of discretion standard…. In determining whether there has been an abuse of discretion, every reasonable presumption should be given in favor of the correctness of the court's ruling…. Reversal is required only where an abuse of discretion is manifest or where injustice appears to have been done…. [This Court] do[es] not … determine whether a conclusion different from the one reached could have been reached…. A verdict must stand if it is one that a jury reasonably could have returned and the trial court has accepted….

Thus, if asked to review the trial court's ruling on a weight of the evidence claim presented to it, an appellate court is not to independently make credibility determinations or assign weight to evidence. Furthermore, [this Court's] task is not to assess the jury's credibility determinations and assessment of weight to evidence. Rather, [this Court's] task is to review, for an abuse of discretion, the trial court's assessment of the jury's credibility determinations and assignment of weight to evidence.

(Brackets in original omitted; quotation marks omitted.) [State v. Capasso, 203 Conn. App. 347-48](toa:capasso); see [State v. Fred C., 167 Conn. App. 606](toa:fred-c).

### 3. The defendant has failed to show that the trial court abused its discretion

The defendant claims that the trial court abused its discretion when it denied his motion for a new trial because it applied an incorrect standard. The defendant seizes upon the trial court's statement that "This Court cannot reject the jury's findings and substitute its own view of the evidence." T.8/16/23: 8. He argues that this statement by the trial court is inconsistent with its obligation, when deciding a motion for new trial, to "do just what every juror ought to do in arriving at a verdict" and "use all his experience, his knowledge of human nature, his knowledge of human events, past and present, his knowledge of the motives which influence and control human action, and test the evidence in the case according to such knowledge" to determine whether the verdict is against the weight of the evidence.

The defendant's argument, however, misreads the trial court's decision, which simultaneously dispensed with a motion for judgment of acquittal and properly stated the standard applicable to that motion. The defendant also fails to account for the trial court's subsequent statement that it "agree[d] with the jury's findings that were based on the evidence presented in this matter…." Id., 9. Read together, and considering the tripartite nature of the trial court's ruling, it is plain that the court did not apply an incorrect standard.

In deciding the defendant's post-verdict motion for judgment of acquittal, the trial court properly stated that it could not "reject the jury's findings and substitute its own view of the evidence." A court deciding a motion for judgment of acquittal is obliged to construe the evidence in the light most favorable to sustaining the verdict. See [State v. Na'im B., 288 Conn. 295-96](toa:naim-b); [State v. Capasso, 203 Conn. App. 345-46](toa:capasso). It is not entitled, however, to "sit as a 'seventh juror'…." [State v. Crewe, 193 Conn. App. 564, 572, 219 A.3d 886, cert. denied, 334 Conn. 901, 219 A.3d 800 (2019)](toa:crewe). To the extent that the trial court was deciding the defendant's motion for judgment of acquittal in its oral ruling, its statement of its inability to reevaluate questions of credibility was correct.

In deciding a motion for a new trial alleging that the jury's verdict was against the weight of the evidence, a trial court applies a different standard. It is permitted to evaluate the credibility of a witness based upon its own observation of the witness' conduct, demeanor, and attitude. See [State v. Capasso, 203 Conn. App. 345-47](toa:capasso). The trial court's decision is in accordance with this standard. As noted, the trial court stated that it "agree[d] with the jury's findings that were based on the evidence presented in this matter…." T.8/16/23: 9. In doing so, the trial court applied the proper standard and evaluated the trial testimony for itself.

To the extent that the court earlier stated that it could not reject the jury's findings and substitute its own views of the evidence, that does not suggest that the trial court applied an improper standard when it denied the defendant's motion for a new trial. Rather, that simply represents that the court applied the correct standard to the motion for judgment of acquittal and then applied a separate, correct standard to the motion for a new trial.[^14] There was no error and, therefore, no abuse of discretion.

For many of the same reasons, even if this Court believes that the trial court applied an improper standard when deciding the defendant's motion for a new trial, any error was harmless. As the trial court expressly stated, it agreed with the jury's findings. Therefore, even if the trial court incorrectly believed that it could not reevaluate the testimony when deciding the motion for new trial, had it done so, it would have reached the same conclusion. That is, because the trial court stated that it agreed with the jury's findings, even had it factored a reweighing of the evidence into its decision, it would have denied the motion for new trial because it agreed with the jury's credibility determinations.
