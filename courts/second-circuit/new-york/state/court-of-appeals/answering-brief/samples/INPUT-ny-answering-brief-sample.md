# Argument

## I. THE APPELLATE DIVISION CORRECTLY HELD THAT THE STATUTE CREATES THE CAUSE OF ACTION

### A. The Standard of Review

This Court reviews the Appellate Division's determination of a question of law de novo. Where the Appellate Division affirmed the Supreme Court on the same ground, the question for this Court is whether that ground is correct. [Placeholder: cite the controlling authority for the standard of review.]

### B. The Text of the Statute Supports the Cause of Action

The statute's text is the starting point. It provides that a person aggrieved by a failure to give notice may recover the damages caused by that failure. The provision does not require proof of intent, and it does not make recovery depend on any showing beyond the failure itself.

Defendant-Appellant reads a separate subdivision as limiting the remedy to administrative relief. That reading cannot be squared with the words of the subdivision, which refer to "any other remedy available at law," a phrase that would have no work to do if the remedy were confined to the agency. [Placeholder: quotation with citation.]

> The remedy provided by this section is in addition to, and not in lieu of, any other remedy available at law.

A court must give effect to every word of a statute. Reading the subdivision as Defendant-Appellant proposes would render that phrase meaningless, and it would reverse the presumption that the Legislature chose its words with care. [Placeholder: cite the canon of construction.]

### C. The Legislative History Confirms the Plain Reading

The legislative history points the same way. The sponsor's memorandum described the bill as a remedy for the very conduct alleged here, and the committee report explained that the private right of action was added to supplement, not to replace, the agency's enforcement power.[^history] Nothing in the history suggests that the Legislature meant to narrow the remedy in the way Defendant-Appellant now urges.

### D. Defendant-Appellant's Policy Arguments Do Not Change the Result

Defendant-Appellant argues that recognizing the cause of action will expose regulated parties to uncertain liability. That is a policy argument, and it is for the Legislature to weigh. The statute already contains the limits that address the concern: a notice requirement that is specific, a cure period, and a cap on damages. Those limits show that the Legislature considered and resolved the policy question. [Placeholder: record or statutory citation for each limit.]

## II. THE RECORD SHOWS NO TRIABLE ISSUE OF FACT ON CAUSATION

### A. Plaintiff-Respondent Made a Prima Facie Showing

On the summary judgment motion, Plaintiff-Respondent submitted the notice, the agreement, and the affidavit of the officer who received the notice. That showing established that the change in services occurred without the written notice the agreement required, and that the change caused the loss claimed in the complaint. [Placeholder: record citations.]

The burden then shifted to Defendant-Appellant to produce evidence raising a triable issue of fact. Defendant-Appellant did not do so.

### B. The Opposing Affidavit Does Not Raise a Triable Issue

Defendant-Appellant relies on an affidavit that states the change in services was made "for operational reasons." The affidavit does not dispute that notice was not given. It does not identify the operational reasons, the date on which they arose, or any document that records them. A conclusory statement of this kind does not raise a genuine issue of fact. [Placeholder: cite the rule on conclusory affidavits.]

### C. The Appellate Division Applied the Correct Test

The Appellate Division asked whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Defendant-Appellant, showed a genuine dispute on each element of the claim. It found that it did not. That is the correct test, and the court's application of it to this record is sound. [Placeholder: cite the test and the Appellate Division decision by name and page.]
