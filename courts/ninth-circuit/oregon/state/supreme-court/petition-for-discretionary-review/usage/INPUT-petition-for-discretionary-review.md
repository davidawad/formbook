# I. INTRODUCTION

Plaintiff John Stuart petitions for review of the decision of the Court of Appeals in *Stuart v. Pittman and Country Mut. Ins. Co.*, 235 Or App 196, \_\_\_ P3d \_\_\_ (2010) (attached). Plaintiff sued for breach of an oral agreement to provide insurance, alleging two alternative theories. The jury found in favor of plaintiff on both theories and returned a verdict in the amount of $268,417. The Court of Appeals reversed the resulting judgment because it concluded there was no evidence in the record to support the verdict.

The Court of Appeals misconstrued the applicable statute, ORS 742.043(1), as requiring evidence that it does not require. The statute provides:

> “Binders or other contracts for temporary insurance may be made orally or in writing, and shall be deemed to include all the usual terms of the policy as to which the binder was given together with such applicable endorsements as are designated in the binder, except as superseded by the clear and express terms of the binder.”

*Id.* (emphasis added).

The phrase “clear and express” modifies “terms of the binder.” The jury found evidence of the binder’s clear and express terms. Because those terms were inconsistent with exclusions in the written policy, they necessarily superseded the exclusions by implication. But the Court of Appeals erroneously treated “clear and express” as modifying “superseded.” It read the statute as requiring evidence that defendant “clearly and expressly waived or superseded the usual terms or exclusions of course of construction [insurance] coverage.” *Stuart*, 235 Or App at 204. It said, “it is plaintiff’s burden to show that those usual exclusions have been explicitly superseded.” *Id.* at 205. ORS 742.043(1) contains no such requirement. Based on that error, the Court of Appeals reversed the verdict for lack of evidence – evidence which the law does not require in order to prove the claim. Viewed in light of the statute as correctly understood, plaintiff’s evidence was sufficient to support the verdict. The Court of Appeals erred in concluding otherwise.

The Court of Appeals also erred with respect to plaintiff’s alternative theory. Plaintiff alleged that defendant’s oral agreement to provide insurance included an implied duty to deliver the written policy within a reasonable time. The insurer failed to issue the policy until long after the loss had occurred. Plaintiff offered evidence that, if defendant had delivered the policy within a reasonable time, plaintiff could have obtained coverage from other sources. The Court of Appeals erred in concluding that there was no evidence to support plaintiff’s alternative theory.

Because the Court of Appeals reversed the verdict based on its conclusion that the verdict was not supported by any evidence, it did not reach defendant’s first four assignments of error. They relate to the standard of proof described in the jury instructions. Defendant asserted that the trial court erred in instructing the jury that plaintiff was required to prove the terms of the oral binder by a preponderance of the evidence rather than by clear and convincing evidence. In the interest of efficiency, plaintiff requests that if the court accepts review, it address those assignments of error and hold that plaintiff was required to establish the clear and express terms of the oral binder by a preponderance of the evidence and not by clear and convincing evidence.

The Court of Appeals also did not reach defendant’s eighth assignment of error. Defendant argued that the trial court erred in awarding plaintiff attorney fees and costs pursuant to ORS 742.061(1) because “any policy of insurance of any kind or nature” does not include an oral insurance binder. Again, plaintiff requests that this court exercise its discretion to dispose of that assignment of error by holding that ORS 742.061(1) applies to an oral insurance binder.

# II. HISTORICAL AND PROCEDURAL FACTS

The Court of Appeals correctly stated the historical and procedural facts.

# III. QUESTIONS PRESENTED AND PROPOSED RULES OF LAW

A. Does ORS 742.043(1) require proof that the usual terms of the policy were explicitly superseded by clear and express terms of the binder, or is it sufficient that the clear and express terms of a binder implicitly superseded the usual policy terms?

Proposed rule: Evidence that clear and express terms of a binder necessarily superseded the usual policy terms by implication is sufficient.

B. Did the trial court err in submitting to the jury plaintiff’s alternative theory, i.e, that the oral agreement to provide coverage included an implied duty to supply the written policy within a reasonable time and breach of that duty kept plaintiff from getting coverage from an alternative source?

Proposed rule: An oral agreement to provide insurance includes an implied duty to provide a written policy in a reasonable time and there was evidence in this case that plaintiff was damaged because he could have obtained alternative coverage but for defendant’s breach of that implied duty.

C. Must a claim for breach of an oral contract for insurance be proved by a preponderance of the evidence, or by clear and convincing evidence?

Proposed rule: A claim for breach of an oral contract for insurance coverage must be proved by a preponderance of the evidence.

D. Does ORS 742.061(1) apply to oral binders of insurance?

Proposed rule: The phrase “any policy of insurance of any kind of nature” in ORS 742.061(1) encompasses oral binders of insurance.

# IV. IMPORTANCE OF ISSUES TO BE REVIEWED

The Court of Appeals ruled that the jury’s finding of liability and its compensatory damages award of more than $268,000 should be set aside. It based this conclusion on an erroneous interpretation of ORS 742.043(1), which governs the enforceability of binders or other contracts for temporary insurance. Such contracts are commonplace. Parties to such contracts need to know what they must do to achieve an enforceable agreement on the terms they intend. After the decision in this case, persons who request and pay for insurance pursuant to the clear and express terms of a binder, may not enforce those terms unless they also explicitly specify that the usual exclusions are superseded.

The decision interjects a requirement not found in the text of the statute and it makes little practical sense, since the detailed terms of the insurer’s usual policy (including exclusions) typically are not even known to the insured at the time of the issuance of the oral binder. *See* ORS 742.043(2) (insurers have 90 days from the date of oral binder to issue policy). The decision creates a standard for including specific, non-standard terms in oral binders that few insureds will be able to meet, effectively negating the standard that the legislature specified in the statute.

Plaintiff’s alternative theory is also worthy of review. Plaintiff alleged and proved an oral agreement to provide insurance. The purpose of such an agreement is defeated if the insurer fails to provide the written policy within a reasonable time. That duty is, therefore, an implied term of the oral agreement. One damaged as a result of breach of an implied duty is entitled to contractual damages. Plaintiff offered evidence that coverage for the loss was available from other sources before the loss occurred; the jury could properly infer that plaintiff would have obtained such coverage and thereby avoided the damages he suffered, if defendant had delivered the written policy within a reasonable time so that plaintiff was aware of the specific exclusions. Plaintiff submits that, if this court agrees with plaintiff that his evidence of damage was sufficient, it should decide that the trial court did not err in submitting plaintiff’s alternative theory to the jury.

If this court accepts review to address either or both of plaintiff’s alternative theories of liability, plaintiff respectfully requests that it clarify two further matters: the quantum of proof is the same for a claim of breach of an oral contract for insurance as for most other contract matters, *viz.*, a preponderance of the evidence; and, ORS 742.061(1) applies to oral binders of insurance.

# V. ARGUMENT

## A. ORS 742.043(1) requires only that a plaintiff prove the clear and express terms of the binder.

Plaintiff offered evidence of clear and express terms of an agreement, or binder, to cover “all instances that something goes wrong during construction.” *Stuart*, 235 Or App at 204. That coverage is inconsistent with the exclusions from coverage contained in the written policy defendant eventually provided to plaintiff after the loss occurred. Those exclusions were, therefore, implicitly superseded by the clear and express terms of the binder. That is all the ORS 742.043(1) requires. The Court of Appeals, however, construed the statute to impose on plaintiff a further burden of showing that the usual exclusions “have been explicitly superseded.” *Id.* The Court of Appeals erred. When the statute is correctly understood to require that the terms of the binder be clear and express, and not to impose a further requirement that they explicitly superseded the usual exclusions, the jury’s verdict was supported by the evidence.

Plaintiff bought a small family farm and improvements in Yamhill County. In March, 2003, he met with Defendant Ronald Pittman, an agent for Respondent-Defendant Country Mutual Insurance Company. Plaintiff told Pittman that he wanted “course of construction” coverage for a new house he planned to build. “Course of construction” insurance is a type of property and liability insurance that people buy to cover a building during construction. Plaintiff told Pittman that he wanted coverage that would provide a “safety net” or catch basin” of coverage, “in all instances that something goes wrong during construction.” Plaintiff testified that, although he did not recall specifically asking for protection for the builder’s failure to perform, he believed Pittman understood that he was seeking such coverage and agreed that defendant would provide it. *Id.* at 198-201.

Pittman orally bound coverage in August 2003, effective September 1, 2003. Plaintiff testified that Pittman did not advise him of any exclusion from coverage. Plaintiff began construction. *Id.* at 199-200.

In January, 2004, the area suffered a severe storm. The contractor had framed the house but not enclosed it, leaving it open to the elements. Snow and ice built up inside the house, causing the interior sheathing to split, water to accumulate in the crawl space, and a large amount of mold. *Id.* at 199.

The Court of Appeals reversed, finding no evidence to support the verdict. The court’s analysis was distorted by legal error, namely, its misinterpretation of ORS 742.043 as requiring not only that the terms of the binder be clear and express, but also that those terms have explicitly superseded the terms of the usual policy. The error is evident in the court’s opinion.

In other words, the Court of Appeals construed the statute as if it said that binders shall be deemed to include the usual terms “except as clearly and expressly, or explicitly, superseded by the clear and express terms of the binder.” But that is not what the statute says. Nothing in the text of the statute precludes a plaintiff from showing that the clear and express terms of the binder necessarily or implicitly superseded the usual terms of the policy.

The Court of Appeals’ construction violates the rule that, in construing a statute, “the office of the judge is simply to ascertain and declare what is \* \* \* contained therein, not to insert what has been omitted \* \* \* .” ORS 174.010. Its construction also makes no sense in light of the context of ORS 742.043(1). The subsection immediately following it, ORS 742.043(2), gives insurers 90 days from issuance of a binder to issue a policy. An insured who does not have a copy of the policy would be hard pressed even to negotiate, much less reach an agreement, explicitly superseding the written policy’s terms and exclusions. The Court of Appeals therefore misconstrued the statute and, in so doing, required plaintiff to offer evidence that the law does not require.

## D. ORS 742.061(1) applies to oral binders.

The trial court awarded plaintiff attorney fees and costs pursuant to ORS 742.061. That statute provides:

> “Except as otherwise provided in subsections (2) and (3) of this section, if settlement is not made within six months from the date proof of loss is filed with the insurer and an action is brought in any court of this state upon any policy of insurance of any kind or nature, and the plaintiff’s recovery exceeds the amount of any tender made by the defendant in such action, a reasonable amount to be fixed by the court as attorney fees shall be taxed as part of the costs of the action and any appeal thereon.”

ORS 742.061(1) (emphasis added). The statutory text confirms that the legislature intended to provide for attorney fees and costs to people who are forced to litigate in order to enforce insurance “of any kind or nature,” including oral binders of insurance.

# VI. CONCLUSION

This court should grant the petition for review and reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals. The court should exercise its discretion to consider not only plaintiff’s breach of contract claims, but also the requisite quantum of proof and the applicability of ORS 742.061(1) to oral binders of insurance.
