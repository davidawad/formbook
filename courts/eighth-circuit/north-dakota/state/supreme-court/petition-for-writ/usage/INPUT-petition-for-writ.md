# [¶ 3] STATEMENT OF THE ISSUES

[¶ 4] Whether the District Court erred by Ordering (E1;R.136) the immediate disqualification of Plaintiff Alan Weigel's attorney Joel Fremstad in this case where Weigel and Albertson are each 50% owners of Veritas Crane LLC, are both pursuing direct and derivative claims against each other, pending motions had been fully briefed, there were hearings scheduled less than one week later, Albertson argued that Weigel never had the authority to retain counsel for Veritas, the District Court assumed it had granted a pending motion to amend, the District Court failed to address relevant case law, and the District Court failed to address prejudice to Weigel and lack of prejudice to Albertson.

# [¶ 5] RELIEF SOUGHT

[¶ 6] Petitioner, Alan Weigel ("Weigel") seeks a Supervisory Writ[^1] from this Court directing that the District Court vacate its Order disqualifying Weigel's attorney Joel Fremstad. (E1;R.136).

# [¶ 7] STATEMENT OF THE CASE

[¶ 8] Weigel and Albertson are each 50% owners of Veritas Crane LLC ("Veritas"). (R.1:¶6). On May 20, 2025, Weigel filed a Complaint with the East Central Judicial District Court, with him and Veritas as Plaintiffs. (R.1). On this same date, Weigel filed a Motion for Temporary Restraining Order ("TRO") with him and Veritas as Plaintiffs. (R.4). The District Court denied the TRO on May 23, 2025. (R.17). The District Court denied the TRO in part because "Albertson has ceased all involvement in Veritas' daily operations and is no longer actively participating in the management or oversight of the company." (R.17:¶9).

[¶ 9] On June 12, 2025, more than two weeks after first raising an allegation of a conflict (R.16), Albertson filed a Motion to Disqualify Joel Fremstad as counsel for Weigel. (R.25). Among other things, Albertson claimed that Weigel was not in fact a 50% owner of Veritas and that he never authorized Joel Fremstad to represent Veritas. (R.25, 26)[^2]. Notably, however, Albertson did not seek any sort of immediate relief to prevent Joel Fremstad from continuing to represent Weigel. On June 12, 2025, Albertson also filed his initial Answer to the Complaint. (R.29).

[¶ 10] On June 26, 2025, James Teigland, also an attorney at the Fremstad Law Firm, on behalf of the Plaintiffs, entered into a Stipulation with counsel for Albertson. (R.35). Among other things, the Stipulation set deadlines for Plaintiffs to respond to the Motion to Disqualify and also addressed how the parties would address Albertson's claim that Weigel had waived attorney client privilege because he had "used his work email account to communicate with his attorneys." (R. 35). The District Court approved this stipulation on June 27, 2025. (R.37).

[¶ 11] On July 2, 2025, Albertson filed an Amended Answer to the Complaint as a matter of right. (R.39).

[¶ 12] On July 3, 2025, Plaintiffs made a Motion to Amend their Complaint. (R.42). The Amended Complaint sought to clarify that "any claims brought by Veritas Crane, LLC are brought derivatively because there is apparent confusion regarding Veritas Crane's role in this matter." (R.44:¶3).

Among other things, the Brief emphasized that Albertson's motion must be denied "because this case is a dispute between the only two members of a closely held limited liability company. And while Veritas is a named party, it is effectively a nominal participant." (R.51:¶1). The Brief also distinguished the primary case relied on by Albertson, *Voracheck v. Citizen's State Bank of Larkin*, 421 N.W.2d 45 (N.D. 1988), where the attorney in that case had, unlike the present case, been counsel for Citizen's State Bank prior to litigation beginning. (R.51:¶¶11-12). Here Fremstad's representation only began in relation to the Complaint and TRO.

# [¶ 54] JURISDICTIONAL STATEMENT

[¶ 55] This Court has authority to issue supervisory writs under N.D. Const. art. VI, §2, N.D. Cent. Code § 27-02-04, N.D. R. App. P. 21, and N.D. R. App. P. 27.

# [¶ 56] ARGUMENT

## [¶ 57] I. THIS COURT HAS BROAD SUPERVISORY JURISDICTION TO RECTIFY ERROR AND PREVENT INJUSTICE.

[¶ 58] This Court's power to issue a supervisory writ is granted by the North Dakota Constitution. N.D. Const. Art. VI, § 2. The Constitution does not limit this Court's remedial powers, but instead broadly grants the Court "such original and remedial writs as may be necessary . . . ." *Id.* As such, this Court has reviewed petitions for supervisory writs on a case-by-case basis. *See, e.g., Heartview Foundation v. Glaser*, 361 N.W.2d 232, 234 (N.D. 1985). Whether a writ is to be granted depends on whether such is necessary to "rectify error and prevent injustice" and there is no "viable alternative remedy." *Id.* *See also Jane H. v. Rothe*, 488 N.W.2d 789 (N.D. 1992); *City of Fargo v. Dawson*, 466 N.W.2d 584 (N.D. 1991); *Burlington Northern, Inc. v. North Dakota Dist. Court, Richland County*, 264 N.W.2d 453 (N.D. 1978) (issuing a supervisory writ vacating an improper discovery order).

[¶ 59] The availability of an eventual appeal is not itself a bar to the grant of a supervisory writ. *Compare State ex rel. Fitzgerald v. District Court*, 703 P.2d 148, 154 (Mont. 1985). As the Montana Supreme Court has explained:

> There are no written regulations or laws respecting our power of supervisory control, and this Court has followed the practice of proceeding on a case-by-case basis although we have been careful not to substitute the power of supervisory control for an appeal provided by statute. We have said, however, that if it is apparent from the record that a relator will be deprived of a fundamental right, both justice and judicial economy require the Supreme Court to resolve the issue in favor of the relator and assume jurisdiction. If the cause in district court is mired in procedural entanglements and appeal is not an adequate remedy, we will issue a writ of supervisory control.

*Id.* (internal citations omitted). As this Court has stated: "Availability of an appeal after final judgment often falls short of sufficient protection, however, as the burden, expense, and delay involved in a trial renders an appeal from a final judgment an inadequate remedy." *Olson v. North Dakota District Court Richland County*, 271 N.W.2d 574, 578 (N.D. 1978); *City of Williston v. Beede*, 289 N.W.2d 235, 236 (N.D 1980) (issuing a supervisory writ is appropriate when "if the writ were not granted something would have been done which probably could not have been undone later"). "As the United States Supreme Court said in *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 388 U.S. 333, 363 (1966): 'reversals are but palliatives; the cure lies in those remedial measures that will prevent injustice at its inception.'" *Olson*, at 578.

## [¶ 60] II. THE RIGHT TO ONE'S COUNSEL OF CHOICE IS FUNDAMENTAL AND CANNOT BE CORRECTED WITH AN ORDINARY APPEAL.

[¶ 61] It is well settled that a civil litigant has the right to the counsel of its choice. *See Thompson v. Goetz*, 455 N.W.2d 580, 587 (N.D. 1990); *Security General Life Ins. Co. v. Superior Court in and For Yuma County*, 718 P.2d 985, 988 (Ariz. 1986); *Hepworth Holzer, LLP v. Fourth Jud. Dist.*, 496 P.3d 873 (Idaho 2021) (We note that there is a First Amendment right to counsel, but it is seldom articulated in that way. It is more appropriately referred to as "a right to counsel of choice."); *Herrmann v. Gutterguard, Inc.*, 199 Fed. Appx. 745, 752 (11th Cir. 2006); *Mitts v. Stamps*, 352 P.3d 1275, 1277 (Okla. Civ. Ct . App. 2014) (stating that the right to choose one's counsel is "fundamental"); *Lawlor v. DVA Renal Healthcare, Inc.*, 3:15:cv-188, at 3 (D. Conn. 2015); *Hemstad Video, Inc. v. Village of Valley Stream*, 409 F.3d 127, 132 (2nd. Cir. 2005). Accordingly, disqualification is contrary to the right to choose one's own counsel to represent them in litigation and the exercise of this Court's supervisory authority is essential to prevent injustice. *See, e.g., Sargent County Bank v. Wentworth*, 500 N.W.2d 862, 871 (N. D. 1993). In this same vein, a writ is appropriate in relation to the disqualification of counsel because the harm of disqualification cannot be corrected with an ordinary appeal. As explained by the Court in *Christensen v. United States Dist. Court*, 844 F.2d 694 (9th Cir. 1988): "Theoretically, Christensen could appeal the disqualification of his counsel after trial. He could not, however, obtain the desired relief on direct appeal because he seeks to be represented by his chosen counsel at trial. Once a new attorney is brought in, the effect of the order is irreversible. If Christensen and his attorney elected to defy the order and then pursue a direct appeal, they would be subject to contempt sanctions and disciplinary action for ethical violations." (Internal citations omitted). *See also Hepworth Holzer, LLP v. Fourth Jud. Dist.*, 496 P.3d 873 (Idaho 2021).

## [¶ 62] III. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION AND ERRED AS A MATTER OF LAW.

[¶ 63] The granting of a writ is appropriate where the District Court abuses its discretion or errs as a matter of law in disqualifying a party's counsel of choice. *See, e.g., Hepworth Holzer, LLP v. Fourth Jud. Dist.*, 496 P.3d 873 (Idaho 2021); *Nevada Yellow Cab Corp. v. Eighth Jud. Dist.*, 152 P.3d 737 (Nev. 2007); *In re RSR Corp.*, 475 S.W.3d 775, 778 (Tex. 2015). *See also Discover Bank v. Romanick*, 2023 ND 172, 996 N.W.2d 314 (granting petition for supervisory writ where Order was contrary to Rules of Civil Procedure); *Pierce v. Anderson*, 2018 ND 131, ¶ 6, 912 N.W.2d 291 (exercising supervisory jurisdiction even though issue could have been raised in later appeal because court's denial of motion contradicted North Dakota law); *Martinson v. Martinson*, 2010 ND 110, 783 N.W.2d 633 (explaining court abuses its discretion when it acts arbitrarily, unconscionably, or unreasonably, when it misinterprets or misapplies the law, or when its decision is not the product of a rational mental process leading to a reasoned determination). The District Court's ruling here rests on precisely that kind of legal error: it misunderstood the nature of derivative actions and who counsel in such a case represents, and then used that misunderstanding as the foundation for disqualification. That is textbook abuse of discretion warranting supervisory relief.

[¶ 64] In his decision, the District Court asserted that "the threshold question is whether Veritas is or was, in fact, Fremstad's client." (R.136:¶5). In incorrectly answering this question yes, the District Court erroneously concluded: "Derivative Claims are brought on behalf of the entity to redress injury to the entity; counsel in such actions represents the entity, not the individual shareholder or member. *See Smithberg v. Smithberg*, 931 N.W.2d 211 (2019)" and that "By signing and prosecuting Veritas's derivative counts, Fremstad necessarily acted as counsel for Veritas." (R.136:¶8 (emphasis added)). While it is correct that derivative actions are brought by a shareholder or member on behalf of a corporation, neither *Smithberg* nor any other case supports the idea that "counsel in such actions represents the entity, not the individual shareholder or member." Similarly, there is no legal support for, nor does the District Court even try to cite a case to support his conclusion that "By signing and prosecuting Veritas's derivative counts, Fremstad necessarily acted as counsel for Veritas." (R.136:¶8).

[¶ 65] The District Court's premise—that by including derivative claims on behalf of Veritas, Mr. Weigel's counsel thereby became counsel for Veritas—rests on a faulty assumption. Courts addressing this precise issue have rejected the idea that filing or prosecuting a derivative claim automatically makes the shareholder's (or member's) lawyer counsel for the entity. Instead, disqualification requires proof of an actual attorney-client relationship with the entity—something wholly absent here. *See Shen v. Miller*, 212 Cal. App. 4th 48, 56–59 (Cal. Ct. App. 2012).

[¶ 66] In *Shen*, the shareholder's lawyer (Walton) simultaneously represented Shen in a derivative action on behalf of Arnon, Inc. and pursued direct claims against Arnon. The defendants argued, much like Albertson here, that Walton was conflicted because by bringing derivative claims he represented both Shen and the corporation. The trial court and California Court of Appeal rejected that argument and affirmed denial of disqualification, holding that "disqualification is not proper unless an attorney-client relationship exists" with the entity, and the mere fact of pursuing a derivative claim does not create such a relationship.

[¶ 67] *Shen* is especially instructive because the company at issue was also a closely held corporation with two 50/50 shareholders locked in a personal dispute. Despite the overlap of derivative and direct claims, the court concluded that Walton represented Shen alone, not the company. That reasoning applies squarely here. The record shows that undersigned counsel was retained by Weigel personally, performed no pre-suit work for Veritas, and received no authorization or consent from Veritas as an entity (Albertson himself notes that Joel Fremstad and Fremstad law do not and cannot represent Veritas Crane as Weigel is only a 50% owner and Albertson has not consented to the retention of Fremstad or Fremstad Law (E.g, R.25, 26)).

[¶ 68] The District Court nevertheless declared that derivative = entity representation, thereby creating a per se conflict. That conclusion ignores the distinction recognized in *Shen*—that in closely held two-owner entities, derivative claims are a procedural vehicle to test intra-owner disputes, not a basis to transform one owner's personal lawyer into counsel for the company itself. Because disqualification requires an actual attorney-client relationship with the entity, not merely the assertion of derivative claims, the court's order rests on an erroneous legal standard and must be vacated. *See also Gonzalez ex rel. Colonial Bank v. Chillura*, 892 So. 2d 1075, 1077–78 (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 2004) (rejecting claim that derivative counsel automatically represents corporation; disqualification denied absent showing of actual attorney-client relationship with entity: "If the mere fact of pursing a derivative claim that belongs to a corporation, but which the corporation has refused to bring, were enough to establish an attorney-client relationship between the corporation and the lawyer for the derivative plaintiff, there would be no way for the derivative plaintiff to ever have conflict-free counsel. As Gonzalez argues, if we accepted the respondents' contention, we would in effect be letting the fox guard the chicken coop; i.e., the corporation would have the ability to control by whom a derivative plaintiff is represented."); *In re Dayco Corp. Derivative Sec. Litig.*, 102 F.R.D. 624, 630 (S.D. Ohio 1984) ("Nevertheless, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that the case law is virtually unanimous in holding that one counsel can represent a stockholder bringing both an individual and a derivative action.")

[¶ 69] The District Court's errors and faulty assumptions carryover into Paragraph 9 of its Order, (R.136), when it looks to N.D.R. Prof. Conduct 1.13(g), which applies to “Organization as Client.” Rule 1.13 does not come into play, because as noted above in a derivative action, the attorney does not represent the entity. Comment 12 to Rule 1.13 actually helps makes this clear as it notes: “ Under generally prevailing law, the shareholders or members of a corporation may bring suit to compel the directors to perform their legal obligations in the supervision of the organization. . . . Such an action may be brought nominally by the organization, but usually is, in fact, a legal controversy over management of the organization.” (Emphasis added). *See also Daily Income Fund, Inc. v. Fox*, 464 U.S. 523, 529 (1984) (reiterating that a derivative suit is one “founded on a right of action existing in the corporation itself, and in which the corporation itself is the appropriate plaintiff.”). Such is what occurred here, Weigel brought suit directly and derivatively, and named Veritas as a nominal plaintiff, but it was only Weigel and not Veritas that had an attorney client relationship with Joel Fremstad and the Fremstad Law Firm. Thus, the District Court's reliance on Rule 1.13(g) reflects a further misunderstanding and misapplication of the law.

[¶ 70] The District Court's errors then continued on into Paragraphs 10 and 11 of the Order where the District Court focused on Rule 1.7. (R.136). Rule 1.7 prohibits representation of a "client" under various circumstances. Again, Weigel is the client and the derivative nature of some of Weigel's claims does not change this. As such, Rule 1.7 does not apply to justify the disqualification of Joel Fremstad or Fremstad Law.

[¶ 71] The District Court's errors then continue on into the penultimate paragraph 12 of the Order. (R.136) The District Court asserts: "Albertson's derivative claims allege injury to Veritas by Weigel, so any recovery would belong to the company and necessarily come at Weigel's expense. This is a zero-sum posture that creates direct adversity and, at a minimum, imposes a material limitation on Fremstad's ability to provide independent and impartial advice. With Weigel and Veritas opposed in the same litigation-and no informed consent from Veritas-Rule 1.7 prohibits Fremstad from representing Weigel. Disqualification is therefore required." Again, the District Court ignores the reality of a derivative action. The District Court also ignores that Weigel's derivative claims allege injury to Veritas by Albertson, so under the District Court's logic, Albertson's own attorneys would also face disqualification. Such of course is absurd, but illustrates the District Court's error and why the District Court's Order cannot stand.

[¶ 72] Also telling is the District Court's Order nowhere mentions *Vorachek v. Citizens State Bank of Lankin*, 421 N.W.2d 45 (N.D. 1988), even though Albertson initially staked his motion to disqualify almost entirely on that case (R.25:¶19–23). In Weigel's opposition and supporting declarations, Weigel and undersigned counsel specifically explained why *Vorachek* is inapposite: in *Vorachek*, the same attorney simultaneously represented a bank and individual defendants accused of siphoning money from that very bank. The North Dakota Supreme Court concluded disqualification was necessary because the attorney was literally on both sides of the same transaction—representing the entity victim and the alleged wrongdoers at the same time. (R.51:5:¶11-15). That situation is categorically different from the present case. Here, Fremstad has never represented Veritas as an entity, has never been retained by Veritas, and has never provided legal advice to its board. The only client is Mr. Weigel, as confirmed by the written retainer agreement and sworn declarations (R.50; R.51). The derivative claims are simply a procedural vehicle to test ownership and fiduciary issues between Weigel and Albertson, not evidence of dual representation. Ignoring Weigel's briefing on this distinction, the District Court effectively treated *Vorachek* as controlling without analysis, even though the core rationale of *Vorachek*—an attorney caught representing both a corporation and its accused wrongdoers—does not apply here. This omission underscores that the Order rests not on a careful application of precedent but on a flawed assumption about the nature of derivative representation.

[¶ 73] North Dakota law recognizes that disputes in closely held entities are fundamentally different from public-company litigation, and courts therefore avoid rigid, formalistic applications that would exacerbate intra-owner strife or weaponize procedure. *See, e.g., Balvik v. Sylvester*, 411 N.W.2d 383, 387–89 (N.D. 1987) (acknowledging the "peculiar problems" of closely held corporations and authorizing tailored equitable remedies to address owner-on-owner disputes); *Red River Wings, Inc. v. Hoot, Inc.*, 2008 ND 117, ¶¶18–26, 751 N.W.2d 206 (same; emphasizing flexible, equity-focused relief in two-owner breakdowns). As the North Dakota Supreme Court has recognized "Courts need not ignore the litigation is really a dispute among shareholders." *Schumacher v. Schumacher*, 469 N.W.2d 793, 798 (N.D. 1991). That pragmatic approach also informs pleading posture: in a two-owner company, derivative counts are often a procedural vehicle to test fiduciary duties and ownership rights while the company appears nominally, not a transformation of one owner's personal lawyer into corporate counsel. Weigel's briefing below explained exactly this point, with record citations showing Veritas functioned as classic two-owner, closely held businesses, and that counsel was engaged by Weigel alone (R.1, R.6, R.47, R.80, R.101, R.121). By treating the mere inclusion of a derivative claim as automatic "entity representation," the District Court's Order ignored North Dakota's closely-held jurisprudence and imposed a result that intensifies the very prejudice those cases caution against—displacing an owner's chosen advocate in the middle of an ownership fight on the eve of dispositive hearings.

## [¶ 74] IV. A WRIT IS WARRANTED TO CORRECT THE INJUSTICE TO WEIGEL

[¶ 75] The prejudice to Mr. Weigel is immediate and irreparable. Disqualification strips him of long-standing counsel of choice on the eve of critical hearings on arbitration and attorney client privilege. This Court has recognized that "availability of an appeal after final judgment often falls short of sufficient protection" when the harm cannot be undone. *Olson v. Dist. Ct. of Richland Cty.*, 271 N.W.2d 574, 578 (N.D. 1978). Here, the burden, expense, and disruption caused by forcing new counsel at this juncture cannot be cured on later appeal. Supervisory intervention is warranted to prevent injustice.

## [¶ 76] V. REASSIGNMENT ON REMAND IS APPROPRIATE TO PRESERVE THE APPEARANCE OF JUSTICE.

[¶ 77] As recognized by the Idaho Supreme Court and United States Supreme Court: “One of the realities presented in rare cases like the this one is that mandamus actions such as the one involved in the instant case have the unfortunate consequence of making the (district court) judge a litigant, obliged to obtain personal counsel or to leave his defense to one of the litigants (appearing) before him in the underlying case. The district judge has unavoidably been made a party to this proceeding and has been represented by independent counsel.” *Hepworth Holzer, LLP v. Fourth Jud. Dist.*, 496 P.3d 873 (Idaho 2021) (quoting *Kerr v. U.S. Dist. Ct*, 426 U.S. 394, 402–03 (1976) (internal quotations omitted). As further explained by the Idaho Supreme Court: “In matters of judicial ethics, appearance and reality often converge as one. *Liteky v. United States*, 510 U.S. 540, 565 (1994) (Kennedy, J., concurring) (citing *Offutt v. United States*, 348 U.S. 11, 14 (1954) (“[J]ustice must satisfy the appearance of justice”). “Thus, while we make no finding that the district court is biased against Hepworth Holzer in this case, to avoid even the appearance of impropriety we order that the administrative district judge assign a new judge to this case upon remand.” *Hepworth*, 496 P.3d at 886. As in *Hepworth*, it is appropriate to assign a new judge on remand.
