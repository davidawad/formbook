# Statement of Issues Presented for Review

I. WHETHER, IN A CASE WHERE THE ORIGINAL DECREE IS SILENT ON SCHOOL CHOICE, THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY AWARDED MOTHER SOLE LEGAL CUSTODY WHERE THE PARENTS’ INTRACTABLE CONFLICT OVER THE CHILD'S EDUCATION CONSTITUTED A SUBSTANTIAL AND MATERIAL CHANGE IN CIRCUMSTANCES THAT RENDERED JOINT LEGAL CUSTODY UNREASONABLE AND NOT IN THE CHILDREN'S BEST INTERESTS.

II. WHETHER THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY DENIED FATHER’S APPLICATION FOR CONTEMPT

III. WHETHER THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY DENIED FATHER’S APPLICATION FOR ATTORNEY FEES AND WHETHER MOTHER SHOULD BE AWARDED APPELLATE ATTORNEYS FEES

# Routing Statement

This case is properly before the Iowa Court of Appeals. The facts and issues do not seek to resolve conflict in existing Iowa law, involve matters of first impression, or otherwise present substantial questions of law. Contrary to Nathan’s belief, the holdings in *In re Marriage of Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d 775 (Iowa 2024) and *Venechuk v. Landherr*, 20 N.W.3d 471 (Iowa 2025), are not in conflict. Moreover, the facts presented in this case are easily disposed of under, and in harmony with, the holdings in *Frazier* and *Venechuk*.

# Nature of the Case

Taylor essentially agrees with the “Nature of the Case” in Nathan’s brief but adds that the District Court also denied Respondent’s Application for Contempt and Request for Attorney Fees, both of which Respondent now appeals in addition to the decision to award Petition sole custody of the children.

#### Procedural History

Taylor Van Kooten n/k/a Taylor Ahrens (“Taylor”) and Nathan Van Kooten (“Nathan”) dissolved their marriage by Decree of Dissolution entered on May 28, 2021. D0016, Decree (5/28/2021). After the parties’ mediation failed to result in a resolution, Taylor filed a Petition for Modification seeking sole legal custody of the children. D0043, Pet. for Mod. (11/15/2024). To help with her issues, B.V.K. was assigned a behavior-focused Individualized Education Plan (“IEP”). D0159, Tr. at 16:22-17:6 and 22:1-25. In accordance with her IEP, special education teacher, Macy Richards (f/k/a Macy Taylor), worked closely with B.V.K. and B.V.K.’s begindergarten teacher, Lisa Huff, throughout the 2023-2024 school year.[^3] D0159, Tr. at 21:10-23, 151:12152:2.

### A. The Iowa Supreme Court’s Holdings in *Frazier* and *Venechuk* Support the District’s Court’s Decision Awarding Taylor Sole Legal Custody

In *Frazier*, the Supreme Court assessed the district court’s authority to decide disagreements over the child’s legal status, medical care, education, extracurricular activities, and religious instructions, in joint custody cases. *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 778. There, a mother filed an “application for vaccination” seeking to have her child receive the Covid-19 vaccination. *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 777. However, she also made clear that she “d[id] not wish to remove or alter [the father’s] status as a joint legal custodian.” *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 782. The district court denied the mother’s application on the basis that the decree gave the parents joint legal custody over the children, finding that “until that provision of the decree has been modified, each party has the right to equal participation in decisions concerning medical care.” *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 778. The court of appeals reversed the district court and ordered the district court to hear the mother’s application on the merits. *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 778. The father then appealed to the Supreme Court.

After a thorough analysis of Iowa law, the Supreme Court found Iowa’s legal custody statute does not allow district courts to “unbundle legal custodial rights to carve out a limited right for one parent to be the sole decision-maker.” *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 786 citing *In re Marriage of Makela*, 987 N.W.2d 467, 471 (Iowa Ct. App. 2022). The Court explained that the statute “treats joint custody as an all-or-nothing proposition that ‘leaves no room for parceling of rights.’” *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 779 quoting *Makela*, 987 N.W.2d at 471. The Court went on to say that “[the] decree was entered nearly ten years ago, and short of a petition to modify the decree to award one of the parent's sole legal custody, the court lacks authority to do anything else.” *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 781 (Iowa 2024).

Following *Frazier*, practitioners and lower courts—including the Court of Appeals in *Venechuk*—initially struggled to define the scope of the rule, leading to inconsistent applications regarding the district court’s authority to resolve joint custody impasses. In *Venechuk*, a mother seeking to move her daughter into a different school appealed from the district court’s denial of her petition to modify the joint custody decree, which incorporated the parties stipulation. *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 472. A split Appellate Court interpreted *Frazier* as requiring every modification request “within the scope of legal custody” to seek “sole legal custody. *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 475. “Because [the mother] never sought to receive sole legal custody, her petition was doomed.” *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 475. The Appellate Court thus affirmed the District Court’s ruling without reaching the merits of the mother’s claims. *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 472.

In reversing the Appellate Court, the Supreme Court meticulously delineated the boundaries of its decision in *Frazier* and in doing so set guardrails on how it should be applied. *See*, *Venechuk v. Landherr*, 20 N.W.3d 471, 472 (Iowa 2025). Specifically, the Court highlighted that the *Frazier* record lacked two critical elements: a decree that “explicitly reserve[d] the district court’s jurisdiction” and a pleading asserting a substantial or material change in circumstances. *Venechuk v. Landherr*, 20 N.W.3d 471, 476 (Iowa 2025) citing *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 781-82. The Court further clarified its prior endorsement of several unpublished appellate decisions that permitted district courts to adjudicate “dispute[s] over a postdecree change of school for a minor child.” *Venechuk v. Landherr*, 20 N.W.3d 471, 476 (Iowa 2025) citing *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 782-84. In this regard, the Court noted that district courts can resolve disputes about where a child attends school “when a party filed a petition to change the school.” *Venechuk v. Landherr*, 20 N.W.3d 471, 476 (Iowa 2025) citing *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 784. (emphasis added). However, this was not the only requirement.

Crucially, the Court reaffirmed that “in the absence of a motion to modify some aspect of the decree—and in *Frazier* there was nothing but the joint legal custody provision to modify—the mother’s motion could not succeed.” *Venechuk v. Landherr*, 20 N.W.3d 471, 476 (Iowa 2025) citing *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 788. (emphasis added). Simply put, when a decree is silent on a specific joint custody right, the court lacks the authority to resolve “free-floating” disputes and may only modify the joint legal custody status itself. *See*, *Venechuk v. Landherr*, 20 N.W.3d 471, 472 (Iowa 2025).

Moreover, in distinguishing *Venechuk* from *Frazier*, the Court explained that “[h]ere, by contrast, the original decree contained a provision stating which school [the child] would attend. That was the provision [the mother] sought to modify, and she could do so without also seeking to obtain sole legal custody.” *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d 471, 476-77 (Iowa 2025). In summarizing the differences from *Frazier*, the Court stated:

> The mother in *Frazier* did not file a petition for modification, and she didn't want to alter the decree. She wanted the courts to referee a medical decision. By contrast, this is a modification case. [The *Venechuk* parents’] custody decree stated that [the child] would attend St. Ansgar; [the mother] wanted to change that. In fact, the decree specifically contemplated court involvement such as occurred here: it required, and thus also foresaw, the possibility of “court approval” to alter the school district.

*Venechuk v. Landherr*, 20 N.W.3d 471, 477 (Iowa 2025) (emphasis added) citing *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 779.

Furthermore, citing the long-standing rules governing judgments, the Court reaffirmed that a decree remains binding and final unless a party successfully petitions for modification based on changed circumstances. *See*, *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 477 (Iowa 2025). This classification of a decree as a judgment underscores that the court’s authority to intervene is limited by the formal rules of finality rather than a general power to manage parental disputes. For these reasons, the Court found that the Appellate Court erred in affirming the district court’s decision without assessing the merits of the case.

When reading *Frazier* alongside *Venechuk*, the Supreme Court’s analysis establishes four critical holdings regarding a district court’s authority to resolve post-decree parenting disputes.

First, to properly invoke a district court’s authority to resolve a dispute involving a joint legal custody right—such as those medical or educational decisions enumerated in Iowa Code § 598.1(3)—a party must file a petition to modify the decree rather than a mere “application for determination.”

Second, the statutory definition of joint legal custody under Iowa Code § 598.1(3) treats custodial rights as a unified bundle that cannot be “unbundled” by the district court. Because parents are entitled to “equal participation” in all decisions involving a child's medical care, education, and religious instruction, a court lacks the authority to carve out a single issue—such as choice of school—and grant one parent sole decision-making power while maintaining the joint custody label for all other matters. Iowa Code Ann. § 598.1(3); *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 786.

Third, a district court’s authority to intervene in a joint custody dispute without having to award sole custody is strictly limited to issues tethered to the decree itself. A court cannot act as a general tiebreaker for daily parenting decisions; however, if the decree contains a specific provision—such as a designated school district or a unique medical protocol—the court maintains the authority to modify that provision upon a proper showing of changed circumstances. *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 477.

Fourth, a party seeking to modify any joint custody provision—including those ‘tethered’ to the decree—must generally prove a substantial change in circumstances. The only exception to this is if the decree itself contains an explicit reservation of jurisdiction that “unequivocally provides for later court review” without the need for a showing of changed circumstances. *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 781; *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 477.

Taken together, these holdings create a cohesive and robust framework that follows the legislature’s intent under Iowa Code section 598.1(3) by safeguarding parental rights while empowering courts to resolve fundamental impasses. This framework is precisely why Nathan’s central argument—that affirming the District Court would require courts to award sole custody every time parents disagree—is a legal fiction. The stringent substantial change of circumstances requirement (the fourth holding) serves as the critical gatekeeper, ensuring courts do not intervene in minor disputes, but only when faced with an unforeseen, permanent or near permanent conflict such that it paralyzes the parents’ ability to co-parent. The prohibition of unbundling (the second holding) complements this by providing structural clarity. It is not a punitive standard designed to aggressively remove parental rights, but a functional one that prevents courts from becoming perpetual referees over single issues. The third holding then carves out the exception to this rule, clarifying that a court can modify a single provision in a decree, without destroying joint legal custody, only when the provision is already explicitly written into the original decree or stipulation.

By treating joint custody as a unified package except in these narrow circumstances, the law directs the court to assess the core problem: whether the ability to engage in shared decision-making has eroded to the point of collapse. This system respects the finality of decrees and forces parents to resolve typical disagreements, reserving judicial intervention as a last resort for the very kind of intractable impasse that the District Court correctly identified here.

Moreover, Nathan’s criticism of the District Court for not citing *Frazier* is not only a red herring, but it is also fundamentally self-defeating. Nathan himself concedes that “*Frazier* does nothing to change the standard by which a modification of joint legal custody is had.” Appellant’s Br. at 19. This admission is fatal to his argument. If *Frazier* merely reaffirmed the long-standing substantial change of circumstances standard, then the District Court’s failure to cite it by name is, at worst, a harmless omission.

In any event, Nathan’s procedural complaint is meritless because the record proves the District Court correctly applied the controlling legal principles from *Frazier* and *Venechuk*. Namely, the District Court’s Order Modifying the Decree contains a sentence that serves as an ample summary of the “no unbundling” rule: “Recent case law does not allow the court to parcel out the right to determine schooling while leaving joint legal custody in place for remaining issues (such as medical, religion, etc.).” D0155, Order Mod. Dec. at 4. This statement unequivocally demonstrates the court understood it lacked the authority to act as a simple tiebreaker on the schooling issue. By recognizing it could not parcel out this single right, the court correctly concluded that its only available path was to address the joint legal custody status itself upon a proper showing of a substantial change in circumstances.

If, for example, the decree here had included a specific provision governing where the children would attend school and reserved the right to later litigate the issue before the district court, then Nathan may have a valid complaint. *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 476-77. However, that is not the case here. The District Court’s decision was therefore not an error of ignorance, but a deliberate application of the correct legal framework, rendering the absence of a formal case citation legally irrelevant.

After discussing *Venechuk*, Nathan employs a rhetorical device to suggest the law is now ambiguous, asking what can be learned and what remains unclear. Appellant Br. at 28. This attempt to sow confusion is unavailing, as the Supreme Court’s decisions in *Frazier* and *Venechuk* necessarily result in cases being sorted into two groups at the outset. Nathan’s analysis fails because he conflates these two distinct groups.

The first group, illuminated by *Venechuk*, applies when the original decree contains a specific provision governing the disputed issue (e.g., naming a specific school district). In such cases, the court has the authority to modify the specific aspect of custody the provision governs allowing for a targeted remedy that does not disturb the joint legal custody arrangement.

The second group, dictated by *Frazier*, governs cases where the decree is silent on the disputed issue. The conflict is therefore not about a specific clause in the decree, but about the fundamental ability of the parents to exercise their joint custodial rights. If that conflict turns into an intractable impasse, then it constitutes a substantial change in circumstances. However, the court cannot invent a “tiebreaker” power it does not have, nor can it “unbundle” one right from the others. Accordingly, the only available remedy under the statute is to address the joint legal custody status itself. *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 476 discussing *Frazier*,1 N.W.3d at 788.

Here, because the parties’ original decree in this case is silent on the issue of school choice, this case falls squarely into the second group. D0016, Decree; D0015, Stip.). The District Court correctly recognized this. It understood that it could not simply “carve” a decision on schooling. *See*, *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 786. Faced with a proven, substantial change in circumstances—the intractable impasse created by Nathan's refusal to co-parent—the court correctly proceeded to the only remedy available: modifying the joint legal custody status to protect the best interests of the children. D1055, Order Mod. Dec. at 8-9.

Accordingly, this entire exercise is a diversion, designed to distract this Court from one simple, dispositive truth: the law is not unclear, and what we learn from this record is that Nathan manufactured the very crisis he now decries. The answer to his parade of horribles—does every disagreement now lead to sole custody?—is, and always has been, a resounding no. Appellant’s Br. at 19. The law is perfectly clear that the gatekeeper is the substantial change of circumstances standard. *In re Marriage of Harris*, 877 N.W.2d 434, 440 (Iowa 2016). A simple disagreement is not a substantial change. But a parent’s complete and unilateral abdication of his duty to engage on a critical issue, forcing a full-blown crisis and leaving the other parent to manage it alone, most certainly is.

His claim of being a reasonable observer of his child’s needs is further shattered by his testimony at trial, where he concocted a fantasy of “95, 99 percent” positive daily reports for B.V.K. (Tr. at 76:17-77:5) in the face of school records documenting near daily, violent outbursts and a report card showing regressing social development. *Supra*, pp. 20-21, 23-24.

A reasonable partner does not unilaterally declare an end to joint decision-making and then weaponize the courts with a contempt filing when the other parent is forced to act alone. His attempt to divert this Court’s attention to other areas where the parties cooperate is unavailing. The law does not require every aspect of a parental relationship to be in ashes to find joint custody untenable; it requires the court to act when a conflict on a single, vital issue paralyzes the parents and harms the child’s best interests. *See*, *Marriage of Rolek*, 555 N.W.2d 675, 677 (Iowa 1996). That is precisely what occurred here, due entirely to Nathan's refusal to act as the partner he now claims to be.

Finally, Nathan heavily cites two of the “*Frazier* approved” unpublished opinions, *In re Marriage of Flick*, 965 N.W.2d 202 (Iowa Ct. App. 2021) and *In re Marriage of Comstock*, 958 N.W.2d 611 (Iowa Ct. App. 2021). Appellant Br. at 25-26. Nathan claims that *Flick* stands for the proposition that “it is not necessary for a decree to contain an explicit provision regarding the legal custody issue in order for the district court to make tie-breaker determinations.” Appellant Br. at 29. It is further claimed that the decree in *Flick* was “silent as to what school the child would attend.” Appellant Br. at 29. While it is technically true the decree does not state the specific school the child is to attend, it does have a provision stating that if the parties could not agree on where to send their children to school, then the court (after attending mediation) reserves jurisdiction to determine what school system the child will be enrolled in.[^9] As for *Comstock*, as the Court noted in *Frazier* that it was distinguishable on account of the fact that it involved the issuance of a temporary order and thus the Appellate Court never engaged in a full discussion of the issue at hand. *Frazier*, 1 N.W.3d at 784.

The District Court saw this situation for what it was: not a simple disagreement, but a unilateral abandonment of co-parenting duties by Nathan on a critical issue, leaving Mother as the only parent actively trying to navigate the crisis. The court’s decision was not a misapplication of *Frazier*; it was a fact-intensive finding that Nathan’s actions had made joint legal custody unreasonable and detrimental to his children’s welfare. To accept Nathan’s argument would be to reward his obstructionism and create a perverse incentive for parents to refuse to engage on difficult issues, knowing they can later run to the court claiming their manufactured “disagreement” does not warrant a change in custody. The District Court correctly refused to endorse such a dysfunctional and dangerous precedent.

### B. The District Court Correctly Found a Substantial and Material Change in Circumstances Necessitating Modification of the Custody Decree

To modify the custodial provisions of a dissolution decree, the moving party must establish by a preponderance of the evidence that (1) there has been a substantial and material change in circumstances since the entry of the original decree, and (2) the requested modification is in the best interests of the children. *In re Marriage of Frederici*, 338 N.W.2d 156, 158 (Iowa 1983). The change must be “more or less permanent or continuous” and must not have been within the contemplation of the district court when it entered the original decree. *Frederici*, 338 N.W.2d at 158. The legal standard used in weighing the evidence is a preponderance of the evidence. *Id.*

“Iowa courts have modified custody when the parents simply ‘cannot cooperate or communicate in dealing with their children.’” *Harris*, 877 N.W.2d at 441 quoting *In re Marriage of Walton*, 577 N.W.2d 869, 870 (Iowa Ct.App.1998). The Court in *Harris* highlighted similar holdings in prior custody cases, including but not limited to: *Marriage of Swenka*, 576 N.W.2d 615, 617 (Iowa Ct.App.1998) (allocating primary physical care to one parent because the parents could “not cooperate and d[id] not respect the parenting or lifestyles of the other”); *In re Marriage of Stanley*, 411 N.W.2d 698, 701 (Iowa Ct.App.1987) (“[t]he continued inability or unwillingness of parents to cooperate is a factor in determining if a custody modification is appropriate.”). Notably relied upon in *Harris* was the decision of *Melchiori v. Kooi*, 644 N.W.2d 365, 368 (Iowa Ct.App.2002) (finding “discord between parents that has a disruptive effect on children’s lives [is] a substantial change of circumstance that warrants a modification of the decree to designate a primary physical caregiver if it appears that the children, by having a primary physical caregiver, will have superior care.).

Here, Nathan fundamentally mischaracterizes the record in arguing that no substantial change occurred. He attempts to frame the issue as a mere disagreement over schooling and in doing so, dismisses B.V.K.’s profound behavioral issues as nothing more than “temporary” and “foreseeable” challenges. This incredibly idealistic and unrealistic view ignores the mountain of evidence detailing a situation that had become untenable. The District Court correctly identified that B.V.K.’s severe, persistent, and worsening behavioral difficulties—and the parents’ resulting intractable conflict on how to address them—constituted a substantial and material change in circumstances not contemplated at the time of the 2021 Decree. Order Mod. Dec. at 7. Yet the seriousness of B.V.K.’s evolving behavioral issues and their effect on her well-being, and the seriousness of the District Court’s ruling seem to escape Nathan, who analogizes the present situation to “whether or not little Johhny plays peewee soccer.” Appellant Br. at 19.

Moreover, when the original Decree was entered, neither the parties nor the District Court could have foreseen the devastating behavioral issues that would engulf their B.V.K.. B.V.K.’s difficulties, while present before schooling, exploded upon her entry into the Montezuma public school system. It similarly could not have been contemplated that B.V.K. would be suspended from begindergarten for kicking a pregnant educator in the stomach. D0159, Tr. at 16:1-2, 18:24-19:5). Nor could Taylor, Nathan, or the District Court have predicted, nor contemplated, that the IEP and special education instructors, while valiant in their efforts, would be unable to help B.V.K. reach a point where she was not taken out of class for outburst every day. D0159, Tr. at 182:16-183:1 and 170:23-171:7.

Nathan’s claim that these challenges were “addressed” misrepresents the record. While dedicated school staff worked to support B.V.K. through an IEP, the situation quickly overwhelmed the available interventions. The record is a painful chronicle showing that despite the support of the IEP and the efforts of her teachers, B.V.K.’s escalating behaviors could not be contained within the school environment. Following her suspension, B.V.K. engaged in a relentless pattern of disruptive and violent conduct, including hitting classmates, throwing objects at students and teachers, kicking staff, and eloping from the classroom. *supra*, pp. 20-21.

The most significant change, however, was Taylor’s inability, as the primary physical custodian, to manage this crisis with Nathan, ultimately creating an unbridgeable gap on the fundamental issue of their children’s education. This paralysis, this inability to reach an agreement on how to best educate B.V.K. while helping her learn how to manage her serious and complex behavioral issues, is the very definition of a profound, permanent, and substantial change in circumstances.

Furthermore, while review of custody matters is de novo, the Supreme Court in *Venechuk* noted that its decision to affirm the district court was “driven in large part by the respect [it] give[s] to the district court’s findings.” *Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 478. It further provided that:

> On intangible matters, such as how best to assure stability in [the child’s] life and whether one can trust [the father] to deliver on his promised follow-through, the district court has a closer perspective than we do. ‘The trial court has the advantage of listening to and observing the parties and witnesses and is in a better position to weigh the credibility of witnesses than the appellate court, which is limited to a written record.’

*Venechuk*, 20 N.W.3d at 478 citing *In re Marriage of Heiar*, 954 N.W.2d 464, 469 (Iowa Ct. App. 2020).

It is undeniable that the District Court found Taylor to be more credible than Nathan. For example, the Court found that Taylor “credibly testified that she believed that since she had primary physical care, she could make the decision to homeschool after discussing it with Nathan.” D0155, Order Mod. Dec. at 8. The Court further found that “Nathan was not nearly as well versed in the educational possibilities as Taylor.” D0155, Order Mod. Dec. at 8. Notably, “Taylor proved that the behavioral issues B.V.K. developed and the changes in her educational needs were not contemplated by the Court at the time the Decree was entered and is a substantial change in circumstances.” D0155, Order Mod. Dec. at 7. To the extent the Court did not specifically state in its decision that it found Taylor more credible, the District Court did note that it “considered all of the evidence at trial, and where it was conflicting, the Court assigns more weight to that evidence judged most believable.” D0155, Order Mod. Dec. at 4-5.

For these reasons, the District Court correctly determined that there had been a material and substantial change in circumstances for the parties. D0155, Order Mod. Dec. at 7.

### C. The District Court’s Decision to Award Sole Legal Custody to Taylor Was Necessary, Proper, and in the Children’s Best Interests

Iowa courts enjoy “wide discretion in providing for the best interests of the children.” *Rolek*, 555 N.W.2d at 679. In child custody cases, the first and governing consideration of the courts is the best interests of the child. Iowa R. App. P. 6.904(3)(n). The critical issue in determining the best interests of the child is which parent will do better in raising the child. *In re Marriage of Riddle*, 500 N.W.2d 718, 719 (Iowa Ct. App. 1993). In determining whether a decision to award sole legal custody, Iowa courts must consider the factors enumerated in Iowa Code § 598.41(3). Moreover, awarding sole legal custody is appropriate when it is “very likely that the parties will not be able to agree . . . on fundamental decisions that must be made in their children’s lives, such as education and medical treatment” *Rolek*, 555 N.W.2d at 677. Here, it is unequivocal that Taylor and Nathan have a disagreement over a fundamental decision – their children’s education. *Id.*

Moreover, as the District Court correctly noted, the objective of a court in making a custody decision is to place the children in “the environment most likely to bring [him/her] to healthy physical, mental, and social maturity. *Phillips v. Davis-Spurling*, 541 N.W.2d 846, 847 (Iowa 1995). The District Court also correctly acknowledged that “preservation of the greatest amount of stability possible is a desirable goal.” *In re Marriage of Hansen*, 733 N.W.2d 683, 696–97 (Iowa 2007).

Nathan’s claim that B.V.K. was “doing phenomenal” in school, especially her kindergarten year, is a gross misrepresentation of the facts, supported only by his testimony, which the Court found less credible than Taylor’s. D0159, Tr. at 95:6-8. Furthermore, his overreliance on BVK’s daily reports and report card from kindergarten puts too much credence into what are essentially highly subjective documents that were completed on some days, less so on others, and sometimes, not at all. Nevertheless, Nathan argues that the daily reports show that she received an 18/18 “on the vast majority of days she was in school,” and that “if she did not score an 18/18 she typically scored a 17/18.” Appellant’s brief at 16. Nathan similarly testified that 90 to 99 percent of the reports were nearly perfect. D0159, Tr. at 76:17-77:5.

Not only does this argument get the math wrong (the real answer is approximately sixty-seven percent) but more importantly, it fails to appreciate the important part of the reports—the substantive comments detailing B.V.K.’s behavioral struggles at school. In this regard, the daily reports from kindergarten include no less than thirty-one days with serious outbursts and/or behavioral issues. *supra*, pp. 23-24. Moreover, B.V.K.’s report card does not demonstrate, or even suggest, that B.V.K. is doing “phenomenal” as Nathan suggests. D0159, Tr. at 95:6-8. Rather, it undeniably proves that her kindergarten educators at Montezuma Community School determined she was regressing with respect to her social development. D1036, Report Card at 2.

Nathan’s argument also ignores the credible testimony and letter from Ms. Huff, who testified that B.V.K. would need to be taken out of class daily. D0159, Tr. at 170:23-171:7. Ms. Huff’s letter dated June 8, 2024, is particularly damaging to Nathan’s claims. D0121, Letter from Lisa Huff at 1-3. To a casual observer, Ms. Huff’s letter makes it clear that the public school setting is not a healthy place for B.V.K. to receive her education. To be clear, this has nothing to do with the quality of the Montezuma Public School District. The record is clear that multiple educators came together and did everything they possibly could for B.V.K. However, the challenging days and behaviors remained. D0121, Letter from Lisa Huff at 2-3.

However, as the District Court correctly found, B.V.K.’s behavior improved when Taylor initially began homeschooling her in the beginning of February 2024. D0155, Order Mod. Dec. at 4. The Court further found that Taylor was able to establish a routine that worked for B.V.K. and eliminated having so many transitions throughout the day. As Taylor credibly testified, B.V.K.’s behavioral challenges “lessened a lot.” D0159, Tr. at 39:10-15. In short, Homeschooling provided B.V.K. with a stable environment that directly benefited her mental health and well-being. Homeschooling is in B.V.K.’s best interests.

Instead of contending with these facts, Nathan attacks Taylor’s efforts at enacting B.V.K.’s homeschool plan by: disparaging her research and understanding of the homeschooling curriculums Appellant’s Br. at 38; criticizing the daily schedule she enacted for B.V.K. *Id.*; pointing out that Taylor missed one parent-teacher conference but omitting the fact she was sick Appellant’s Br. at 10; belittling Taylor’s own educational background Appellant’s Br. at 17; and baselessly claiming Taylor’s husband Frank as the reason behind homeschooling B.V.K. Appellant’s Br. at 37—just to name a few. Of course, these ad hominem attacks are legally irrelevant, but worse for Nathan, they support the District Court’s finding that Taylor was the more credible of the two parents to testify, adding credence its decision to award Taylor sole custody the parties’ children.

Nathan’s argument that he communicates well with Taylor and is a good dad are further unavailing because they fail to contend with the reality that he and Taylor are incapable of coming to a consensus on their children’s education. *See*, *Kocinski v. Christiansen*, 2021 WL 5106051, at \*5 (Iowa App., 2021). In *Christiansen*, like in the case at bar, had a minor child that unfortunately struggled mightily with emotional and behavior issues that required medical and/or educational assistance. *Christiansen*, 2021 WL 5106051, at \*1. Also analogous is the fact that the father argued breakdowns in communication [with his ex-wife] were rare.” *Christiansen*, 2021 WL 5106051, at \*5. However, as the Appellate Court pointed out, the reality was of the record in *Christiansen* demonstrated that the parties were “incapable of coming to a consensus on [the minor child’s] course of treatment.” *Christiansen*, 2021 WL 5106051, at \*5. Most notably, the Court distinguished the facts in *Christiansen* from other cases where the court refused to sever joint legal custody:

> We acknowledge that many cases reflect a difficulty in communication between parents; however, the special medical and educational needs of C.B.K., along with the parents’ inability to agree on educational and medical issues, separates this case from others.

*Christiansen*, 2021 WL 5106051, at \*5 (Iowa App., 2021). The Court consequently awarded the mother sole legal custody.

The Court should follow the *Christiansen* Court, and affirm the District Court’s decision, which did the same thing here.

#### Conclusion

The framework established by *Frazier* and *Venechuk* requires courts to sever joint custody in cases like the one at bar. Moreover, the complete inability of the parents to communicate about a fundamental issue—B.V.K.’s education—constitutes a substantial change in circumstances under well-established Iowa law. The District Court acknowledged the same. Finally, as it was mandated to do so, the District Court reviewed all of the testimony and evidence and made a determination that it was in the best interest of the children for Taylor to be awarded sole legal custody. For these reasons, this Court must affirm the District Court’s decision granting Taylor’s Petition for Modification and awarding her sole custody.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY DENIED NATHAN’S APPLICATION FOR CONTEMPT

#### Preservation of Error

Taylor has no objections to preservation of error.

#### Standard of Review

Taylor does not agree with the standard of review set forth by Nathan. In general, a district court’s denial of an application for contempt is reviewed under a gross abuse of discretion standard. *In re Marriage of Swan*, 526 N.W.2d 320, 327 (Iowa 1995).

#### Argument

Filing this contempt application is the legal equivalent of telling someone, “Go ahead, jump in a lake,” and then suing them for getting wet. Nathan explicitly texted Taylor, “[y]ou do what you do, and I’ll do what I have to do.” D0133, Resp. Ex. H, Texts Between Parties, at 5. At trial, he clarified what he meant. “[B]asically all it was, was that if you want to do it, do it.” D0155, Tr. at 136:7-14. Now he is before this court expressing shock and demanding punishment because she did, in fact, do what she had to do. If this Court does what it has to do, then the District Court’s decision on this matter will be affirmed.

Nathan’s contempt argument fundamentally fails for the additional reason that it glosses over the most crucial element required for a finding of contempt: willfulness. Citing *Felton*, Nathan notes that willfulness requires evidence of a “bad or evil purpose” or a “wanton and in disregard of the rights of others.” Taylor’s act in homeschooling B.V.K. meet neither of these definitions. *Felton v. Iowa District Court for Polk Cnty.*, 2023 WL 1809820, at \*4 (Iowa App., 2023).

The District Court, which had the unique advantage of observing the witnesses firsthand, made a specific factual finding that Taylor’s testimony on this issue was credible. Order Mod. Dec. at 8. The court accepted her explanation that she acted not in defiance of the decree, but under the good-faith belief that as the primary physical caregiver, she could make the necessary educational decision for her child. Order Mod. Dec. at 8. A finding of contempt requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of a “bad or evil purpose.” *Felton*, 2023 WL 1809820, at \*4. An act taken under a credibly held, good-faith belief cannot, by definition, meet this extraordinarily high standard. Accordingly, this Court should affirm the District Court’s decision denying Nathan’s Application for Contempt.
