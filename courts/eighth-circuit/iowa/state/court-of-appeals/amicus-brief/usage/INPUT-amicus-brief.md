# INTEREST OF AMICI CURIAE

Senate Majority Leader Mike Klimesh hereby files this amicus curiae brief. Senate Majority Leader Klimesh is joined by fourteen other Iowa State Senators who were duly elected by the citizens of their several districts. The brief is being filed pursuant to Iowa Code § 625A.19 (2026) as the appeal includes a challenge to Iowa Code § 481A.38 as being unconstitutionally vague.

The Senate is a legislative body of the General Assembly created by Iowa Const. art. III. As fifteen elected State Senators, Amici have a duty under the Constitution to ensure that the General Assembly's authority in passing legislation is protected. This case involves a substantial impact on the authority of the General Assembly as a coequal branch of government.

Amici include: Mike Klimesh, Senate Majority Leader; Amy Sinclair, Senate President; Ken Rozenboom, Senate President Pro Tempore; Lynn Evans, Assistant Majority Leader; and Senators Kevin Alons, Mark Lofgren, Charlie McClintock, Jeff Reichman, Dave Rowley, Jason Schultz, Tom Shipley, Annettee Sweeney, Cherielynn Westrich, Jack Whitver, and Dan Zumbach.

# ARGUMENT

## 1. Introduction.

Amici file this brief in defense of Iowa Code § 481A.38 (2026) that sets out prohibited acts on taking wildlife. This Court must first determine if the issue of vagueness has been preserved for appeal. Amici do not argue this point but rather focus this brief on whether the statute is ambiguous. Amici do not believe the law is ambiguous and urge the Court to reject the vagueness challenge. See e.g. *State v. Rhodes*, 6 N.W.3d 741 (Iowa 2024). If the Court does determine the statute is ambiguous, Amici urge the application of Iowa Code § 4.6 (2026) on interpreting ambiguous statutes and court precedent to find that § 481A.38 is not unconstitutional under the void-for-vagueness doctrine. See e.g. *State v. Geddes*, 998 N.W.2d 166 (Iowa 2023).

## 2. Iowa Code § 481A.38 Is Not Vague.

Amici first urge this Court to adhere to the principle of judicial restraint and avoid deciding constitutional issues when a case may be resolved on other grounds. See e.g. *State v. Kieffer*, 17 N.W.3d 651 (Iowa 2025); and *State v. Button*, 622 N.W.2d 480 (Iowa 2001). As such, if the Court can resolve this appeal by avoiding the vagueness issue it should do so. If the Court determines the statute is ambiguous and the issue is preserved, Iowa Supreme Court precedent clearly favors the law's constitutionality.

“The polestar of statutory interpretation is to give effect to the legislative intent of a statute.” *State v. Schultz*, 604 N.W.2d 60, 62 (Iowa 1999). To ascertain legislative intent, the Iowa Supreme Court has stated that it examines the language of the statute, the underlying purpose and policies, the consequences stemming from different interpretations, and that the statute should be construed in its entirety. *Iowa Comprehensive Petroleum Underground Storage Tank Fund Bd., v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 606 N.W.2d 359, 363-365 (Iowa 2000).

The intent of § 481A.38 is that “transport” does not mean striking a deer with a vehicle, prohibiting a hunter from driving to and from a hunting site, or carrying a lawfully taken carcass. The accompanying administrative rule further conveys this intent. Iowa Admin. Code r. 571-106.7(6) provides that the “use of…automobiles, aircraft, or any mechanical conveyance or device…is prohibited.” The rule then provides an exception for “paraplegics and single or double amputees” to “hunt from any stationary motor-driven land conveyance.”[^1] Amici urge this Court to find that the legislative intent is to prohibit the active use of an automobile during the actual hunt. Iowa Code § 481A.1(33) (2026) supports this by defining “transport” as “all carrying or moving or causing to be carried or moved.” Again, the intent is to prohibit certain methods of taking wildlife including impermissible usage of automobiles to do so.

Certain principles are important to note. First, statutes are “cloaked with a presumption of constitutionality” that must be rebutted with proof of “unconstitutionality beyond a reasonable doubt.” *AFSCME Iowa Council 61 v. State*, 928 N.W.2d 21, 31 (Iowa 2019) (quoting *State v. Seering*, 701 N.W2d 655, 661 (Iowa 2015)). See also *In re Detention of Schuman*, 2 N.W.3d 33 (Iowa 2024); and Iowa Code § 4.4 (2026) in “enacting a statute, it is presumed that [c]ompliance with the Constitutions of the state and of the United States is intended.” The argument that the law is vague does not meet the exacting threshold set by statute and judicial precedent.

Second, courts look for a reasonable interpretation that best achieves the statute's purpose as well as looking to utilize interpretations that avoid absurd results. See e.g. *State v. Gonzales*, 718 N.W.2d 304 (Iowa 2006) and *Harden v. State*, 434 N.W.2d 881 (Iowa 1989). To interpret the statute as the Appellant seeks is contrary to this Iowa Supreme Court precedent as that interpretation creates an “absurd result.” Such precedent protects the legislature's lawmaking authority.

Finally, as the Iowa Supreme Court has held, a challenge asserting that statutes are unconstitutionally vague face a heavy burden. See e.g. *State v. Keene*, 629 N.W.2d 360 (Iowa 2001) and *State v. White*, 545 N.W.2d 552 (Iowa 1996). Under judicial precedent, this appeal fails to overcome that heavy burden. As such, this Court should find § 481A.38 constitutional as written by the legislature.

In applying the void-for-vagueness doctrine as established by the Iowa Supreme Court, the legislature enacted a law that meets the criteria. First, fair notice and warning exists. See e.g. *State v. Baker*, 688 N.W.2d 250 (Iowa 2004); and *State v. Millsap*, 704 N.W.2d 426 (Iowa 2005). The statute and rule are part of a comprehensive regulatory scheme on “method of taking wildlife” including the use of automobiles. This fair notice is reflected in the titles and language of both the statute and administrative rule. Hunters have ordinary understanding of what conduct is prohibited. Also, this Court can find that the Appellant's conduct is within any statutory construction and thus the statute is not vague. See *State v. Musser*, 721 N.W.2d 734 (Iowa 2006). Speculative hypotheticals do not change this outcome.

In addition, the law and rule provide sufficient guidance to prevent exercise of power in an arbitrary or discriminatory fashion as related to the methods of taking wildlife. See *State v. Nail*, 743 N.W.2d 535 (Iowa 2007). There are clearly established standards for permitted methods of taking wildlife. This appeal can be resolved on this point without a finding the law vague.

Finally, § 481A.38 does not “sweep so broadly as to prohibit substantial amounts of constitutionally-protected activities, such as speech….” *Id.* at 539. This Court can certainly interpret the statute in a manner that avoids overbreadth. See *Formaro v. Polk County*, 773 N.W.2d 834 (Iowa 2009).

The meaning of the statute is clear and there is no reason for this Court to seek a different meaning than what the text provides. See *State v. Iowa Dist. Ct. for Johnson Cty.*, 730 N.W.2d 677 (Iowa 2007). The accompanying administrative rule supports the intent of the statute. Amici do not believe the law is ambiguous, and certainly not unconstitutionally vague. However, if the Court determines the law is ambiguous, based on the language of the law and rule, as well as the precedent of the Iowa Supreme Court, Amici urge the Court to find that Iowa Code § 481A.38 does not violate the void-for-vagueness doctrine. Such a result protects the legislature's authority under Iowa Const. art. III to pass laws.

# CONCLUSION

This brief is filed pursuant to Iowa Code § 625A.19 as it involves a challenge to the constitutionality of a law. Amici do not request time if oral arguments are scheduled. For the reasons stated herein, Amici urge the Court to find that Iowa Code § 481A.38 is not unconstitutionally vague.
